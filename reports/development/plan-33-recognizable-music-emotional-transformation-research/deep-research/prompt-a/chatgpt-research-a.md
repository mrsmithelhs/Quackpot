# Melody identity under re-arrangement, and arrangement-level tension

## Executive summary

The strongest evidence supports a practical separation between **tune identity** and **arrangement-level affect**. **[E]** Familiar melodies remain identifiable across changes in harmony, key, tempo, and timbre, although those “surface” changes can measurably reduce recognition, especially over short retention intervals; preserving pitch contour/interval relations and rhythm is much more important than preserving the original accompaniment. Kuusi’s familiar-tune study found almost identical recognition for melody-alone versus melody-plus-harmony conditions within each expertise group, while removing pitch information caused much larger losses. citeturn950274view1turn128843view0 **[E]** Liu likewise found that adults could judge a melody as the “same” after substantial reharmonization, although more remote/secondary-chord harmonizations made this harder. citeturn238978view4turn238978view5

For increasing unease while keeping a tune recognizable, the best-supported arrangement levers are **roughness/inharmonicity, dissonance and harmonic instability, lower register, loudness, timbral/orchestral change, and delayed/weak closure**. Direct evidence for drones, irregular pulse, texture density, and sub-bass specifically is thinner. citeturn486534view2turn486534view3turn128843view4turn128843view5turn577253search0

## Identity under re-arrangement

**[E; S1] The clearest directly relevant result is Kuusi’s.** With first-two-phrase excerpts of familiar tunes, changing from melody alone to melody embedded in four-part harmony hardly reduced identification. Professionals recognized **76.1%** of melody-alone versions and **81.3%** of melody+harmony versions; amateurs **67.4% vs 68.0%**; nonmusicians **53.3% vs 53.9%**. By contrast, pitch-stripped rhythm conditions collapsed recognition much more severely: for professionals, rhythm and rhythm+harmony produced **50.5% and 37.6%** recognition; amateurs **15.6% and 8.5%**; nonmusicians **2.5% and 18.6%**. Thus, for these familiar tunes, preserving melodic pitch structure mattered substantially more than preserving or adding harmony. citeturn950274view1

**[E; S2–S3] Reharmonization normally does not erase melodic identity, but harmonically remote changes impose a cost.** Liu’s 2018 study asked 80 adults whether melody pairs were the same after harmony was added, changed, or removed. Participants could “conserve” melody under all these transformations, but were significantly better when harmony moved from primary to other diatonic chords than when it moved to secondary chords. citeturn238978view4 The cross-cultural replication found the same broad ability in 131 Chinese participants and the earlier U.S. sample, with the Chinese group performing significantly better overall. citeturn238978view5 This is unusually close to the intended composition problem because **the dependent variable is explicitly “same melody or different?” under changed harmonization**.

**[E; S5] Key, tempo and timbre are remembered, but they are not necessary for melody identity.** Schellenberg and Habashi changed unfamiliar melodies by six semitones, 64 BPM, or piano↔saxophone timbre. Key and tempo changes reduced recognition after 10 minutes and one day but no longer did so after one week; timbre changes continued to impair recognition at all delays. That means arrangement-level changes can reduce *recognition strength* without changing the underlying melodic identity. citeturn128843view0

**[E; S4, S6] Contour alone is insufficient; local intervals/rhythm and surface cues matter.** Mélen and Deliège’s transformations showed that recognition was best when useful local cues survived, not simply when either global contour or deep harmonic structure was preserved. citeturn238978view6 Factorial melodic-similarity experiments likewise show contributions from contour, tonality, rhythm and meter rather than one cue acting alone. citeturn588704search24

**[E; theme-and-variation evidence]** Dowling and colleagues’ “abstraction of themes from melodic variations” work showed that listeners could abstract a prototype from systematically varied melodies and later treat that prototype as the common theme; in one experiment, listeners who heard the prototype directly behaved similarly to listeners who had inferred it from variations. citeturn588704search1turn588704search30 This supports the broader proposition that identity can persist despite substantial surface variation, although those transformations changed the melody itself rather than merely its accompaniment.

**[E; cover-version evidence]** Naturalistic cover-song similarity studies find that listeners perceive an original and its cover as the same underlying song despite changes in tempo, key, voice and timbre; however, larger tempo differences in particular predict lower similarity ratings. citeturn588704search4turn588704search26 This is convergent rather than clean causal evidence because covers usually change several features simultaneously.

**[H] Composition implication:** keeping the **ordered pitch contour, characteristic interval pattern, characteristic note-duration ratios/rhythm, and salient phrase boundaries** unchanged should give substantially more identity protection than keeping the original harmony, instrument, register, or exact tempo. The evidence does **not** establish a numerical “safe” limit for how far harmony, tempo, bass register or timbre can be changed before a newly composed game cue ceases to feel like the same tune. That threshold is **no evidence found**.

## Fixed-melody arrangement changes and tension

The literature is much thinner here than for melody recognition: many tension studies manipulate a whole melodic or harmonic stimulus rather than holding one melody literally fixed while changing only accompaniment.

**[E; S7] Orchestration by itself can change tension.** Silvey used the same *Bydlo* movement in full-orchestra, brass-quintet and piano orchestrations while matching amplitude, frequency and duration. Continuous tension ratings changed with orchestration, and the direction depended on listener training: musicians rated piano as least tense, whereas nonmusicians rated it most tense. citeturn486534view4 This directly supports arrangement-level control of tension, but it also warns that a single timbre mapping does not generalize across listener groups.

**[E; S8] Register and loudness have large tension effects and interact with tempo and contour.** Across 132 listeners, lower register increased overall tension, as did increased loudness; tempo did not have a simple main effect on overall tension but did affect perceived tension change and interacted with loudness, contour and register. citeturn128843view4 This is strong evidence that a “dire” arrangement using lower accompanying material can work, but it also shows why loudness must be controlled before attributing the effect to orchestration.

**[E; S9–S10] Rougher and more inharmonic timbres reliably raise tension.** Farbood and Price found the strongest evidence for roughness, inharmonicity and spectral flatness among the timbral attributes tested. citeturn486534view2 Pressnitzer et al. independently found that listeners’ tension ratings of nontonal orchestral chords covaried with psychoacoustic roughness. citeturn486534view3 These are good candidates for changing accompaniment while leaving melody intact.

**[E; S11–S12] Weak or thwarted closure is perceptually real, but “deceptive cadence = scarier” is not established.** Listeners differentiate authentic, half, deceptive and evaded cadential structures in their closure expectations; half cadences produce notably weak/late expectations of completion. citeturn128843view6 In Smit et al., cadence type had only small effects on arousal, while mode, pitch height and added dissonance affected valence more clearly. citeturn128843view5turn393417view0 Thus, **withholding tonic closure is well supported as a way to prolong unresolved expectancy**, but direct evidence that it produces “threat” or “dire unease” is limited.

**[E; S13] A sustained drone can alter the affective interpretation of otherwise identical pitch material through tonal context.** In Friedman et al., pseudo-melodies used notes from the same C-major pitch collection while a low continuous drone established different modal centers. Modes placing a minor third above the drone were judged more tense than major-third modes. citeturn238978view0 This demonstrates that a drone can alter tension without changing the pitch inventory, but the ordered melody itself was not fixed, so it is not a direct test of your intended technique.

## Under-tested devices

**Sustained drones and pedal tones. [E; S13]** There is empirical evidence that a drone can shift modal/tension interpretation. citeturn238978view0 **No evidence found** in the searched literature for a controlled experiment in which one fully specified melody was repeated unchanged over different sustained pedal tones and listeners rated unease or threat. The nearest evidence establishes tonal-context effects, not an intrinsically ominous effect of “drone-ness.”

**Sub-bass or very low accompaniment. [E; S8]** Lower pitch register increased overall tension in Granot and Eitan’s experiment. citeturn128843view4 **No evidence found** isolating synthesized **sub-bass accompaniment below a fixed melody** as the manipulated variable. Therefore “add sub-bass to make it threatening” is **[H]**, not directly established by the cited work.

**Detuning, beating and roughness. [E; S9, S10, S14]** This is one of the better-supported devices. Higher roughness/inharmonicity raises judged musical tension, and scream-like horror music contains elevated modulation-spectrum roughness associated with more negative valence and higher arousal. citeturn486534view2turn486534view3turn577253search0 **[H]** Slightly detuned accompaniment voices, clustered intervals or beating synth partials are therefore reasonable ways to introduce unease while leaving the lead melody unchanged, provided the effect is not so spectrally dense that it masks the melody.

**Withheld/deceptive cadences and delayed resolution. [E; S11–S12]** Half, deceptive and evaded cadential contexts alter expected closure timing and stability. citeturn128843view6turn393417view0 The evidence is strongest for **expectancy/closure**, weaker for “unease,” and absent for threat-specific ratings under a fixed melody. **[H]** Reharmonizing the end of the same melodic phrase so that its final melodic note is supported by dominant, deceptive, suspended or non-tonic harmony is therefore an evidence-informed way to make the arrangement feel less settled, but the exact emotional strength must be tested in the target audience.

**Metric ambiguity or irregular pulse. [E]** A recent study found systematic tension fluctuations within regular 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 meters in 35 experienced musicians, showing that metrical hierarchy can influence tension. citeturn178736search5 Martens’ tapping data also show substantial inter-listener ambiguity in tactus selection, with musical training affecting tapping strategy. citeturn178736search6 **No evidence found** from the searched literature directly showing that adding irregular pulse or metric ambiguity beneath one unchanged melody increases unease/threat ratings.

**Sparse versus dense texture. [E]** Sears et al. found denser Mozart cadential excerpts received higher “ending imminent” expectancy ratings overall, but texture was a naturally covarying property of the excerpts rather than an isolated causal manipulation. citeturn128843view6 **No evidence found** for a clean fixed-melody sparse-vs-dense accompaniment experiment using tension or threat as the dependent variable. Claims that “denser is always tenser” are therefore unsupported.

**Dynamic/loudness confounds. [E; S8]** Increased loudness is itself a powerful tension cue and interacts with register, contour and tempo. citeturn128843view4 This is crucial methodologically: if your “dire” arrangement has more layers, more bass and more distortion but is also louder, any informal listening test cannot tell which arrangement variable caused the increased tension. **[H]** For comparing versions, loudness-normalize them first, then reintroduce intentional dynamics only after choosing the arrangement.

## Cultural and training limits, and horror/threat-score acoustics

**[E; S1] Musical training changes which cues listeners can use.** Kuusi’s professionals extracted far more identity from rhythm-only versions than amateurs or nonmusicians, whereas the gap was smaller when melodic pitch information remained. citeturn950274view1 This means an arrangement that weakens the melodic line while assuming listeners can reconstruct the theme from rhythm may work for trained musicians and fail for general players.

**[E; S3] Cross-cultural melody conservation appears robust but not identical.** Both Chinese and U.S. listeners preserved melody identity through harmonic changes, yet Chinese listeners performed significantly better overall in Liu’s comparison. citeturn238978view5 The study therefore argues against treating Western-harmony results as culture-free perceptual constants.

**[E; S7] Training can even reverse orchestration effects.** In Silvey’s study, musicians and nonmusicians disagreed about which orchestration of *Bydlo* was most tense. citeturn486534view4 Consequently, timbre/orchestration findings are especially risky to universalize.

**[E; S11–S12] Cadence and tonal-expectancy effects are partly enculturation-dependent.** Both Smit et al. and Sears et al. use Western tonal cadences; Smit’s participants were German university students, and Sears’ stimuli came from Mozart. citeturn393417view0turn128843view6 These findings are directly relevant to Western-tonal game music but should not be presented as culture-universal.

**[E; S14] Trevor, Arnal and Frühholz provide the most targeted primary evidence for threatening-film-score acoustics.** They analyzed 100 horror-score excerpts—50 selected as scream-like and 50 controls—from ten post-2010 films, all 800 ms and RMS-normalized, alongside 100 vocal clips. Twenty adults rated all sounds. Scream-like music was acoustically rougher and was rated as more negatively valenced and more arousing; within the music excerpts, roughness significantly predicted both lower valence and higher arousal. citeturn577253search0 The study does **not** show that roughness alone caused fear because the music categories were selected rather than experimentally generated, a limitation the authors themselves note. citeturn577253search0

**[E; S15] Blumstein, Davitian and Kaye provide complementary corpus evidence.** Their film-score analysis found systematic genre differences in nonlinear acoustic analogues: horror films had more non-musical sidebands and noisy screams than expected, while other genres showed different profiles. citeturn981636view2 Because this was acoustic/content coding rather than a listener experiment, it establishes **what horror soundtracks contain**, not the causal perceptual effect of each device.

**[E; S16] Bullerjahn and Güldenring show that arrangement can strongly redirect narrative interpretation.** More than 400 listeners watched an approximately ten-minute experimental film with one of five soundtrack versions differing in style, orchestration, motifs and placement. The music altered emotional atmosphere and judgments about characters and plot. citeturn486534view6 It is strong ecological evidence that soundtrack arrangement matters, but weak evidence about the causal contribution of any specific acoustic feature.

**[T; S17] Lipscomb and Tolchinsky should be treated as synthesis, not as a primary “threat-acoustics” paper.** Their chapter reviews empirical and theoretical work on film-music communication and how music changes narrative interpretation. citeturn577253search4 It is useful framing, but it does not supply a single controlled stimulus set or sample size that can answer the present acoustic-feature question.

**[H] For the requested three-version game cue, the evidence-supported hierarchy is therefore:** preserve the melody’s contour, interval pattern and characteristic rhythm across all three versions; then increase tension progressively through reharmonization that weakens closure, lower accompaniment register, rougher/less harmonic timbres, denser dissonant voicings, and carefully controlled loudness. Drones, irregular meter and sub-bass are plausible additional devices, but the direct evidence for those exact fixed-melody manipulations is weaker. citeturn950274view1turn238978view4turn128843view4turn486534view2turn128843view6

## Where the evidence is thin or absent

**[E/H]** The main gaps are not about whether arrangement affects perception—it plainly does—but about **factor isolation under a literally unchanged melody**. The search located **no evidence found** for a controlled fixed-melody experiment manipulating only sub-bass; only pedal-point/drone pitch; only irregular meter; or only sparse-versus-dense accompaniment while collecting explicit threat/unease ratings. The available evidence for those devices comes from nearby paradigms: global register manipulations, modal drones, metrical-tension studies, naturalistic excerpt analyses or multivariable film-score comparisons. citeturn128843view4turn238978view0turn178736search5turn486534view6

**[E]** Precise “identity-loss curves” are also scarce. Kuusi provides useful recognition percentages, but the melody conditions altered rhythm to equal note durations and used familiar songs; Liu shows successful conservation under reharmonization but the accessible abstract does not provide condition-level accuracy or effect sizes. citeturn950274view1turn238978view4 **No evidence found** for a study that parametrically increases accompaniment dissonance, bass depth, timbral roughness or tempo difference and estimates the point at which a newly learned tune ceases to be judged “the same tune.”

**[E]** Horror-score corpus work isolates associations better than causes. Trevor et al. relate roughness to affect ratings, while Blumstein et al. document nonlinear acoustic features by genre, but neither is a fixed-melody arrangement experiment. citeturn577253search0turn981636view2 **No evidence found** that a specific horror arrangement technique such as “low drone + detuned upper cluster + unresolved cadence” has been factorially decomposed in one listener experiment.

## Most decision-relevant sources to read in full

1. **Kuusi (2009), “Tune Recognition from Melody, Rhythm and Harmony.” [E]** This is the most directly actionable identity paper because it separates pitch melody, rhythm and harmony and supplies actual recognition percentages across expertise groups. It shows that adding harmony to an intact melodic contour barely changed familiar-tune identification, while stripping pitch information devastated it. citeturn950274view1

2. **Liu (2018), “An Investigation of Adult Listeners’ Conservation of Melody Under Harmonic Deformations.” [E]** This is the closest match to the core design question: same/different melody judgments under added and changed harmonization. It supports aggressive reharmonization as compatible with retained identity, while indicating that harmonically remote changes carry more risk. citeturn238978view4

3. **Granot & Eitan (2011), “Musical tension and the interaction of dynamic auditory parameters.” [E]** It gives direct experimental evidence for lower register and loudness as tension cues and, importantly, shows that these cues interact with tempo and contour rather than summing independently. That is highly relevant when designing “uneasy” and “dire” arrangement layers. citeturn128843view4

4. **Farbood & Price (2017), “The contribution of timbre attributes to musical tension.” [E]** This is the cleanest source for turning synthesizer sound design into an experimentally supported tension dimension: roughness and inharmonicity are more defensible levers than generic claims about “dark timbre.” citeturn486534view2
