# Keeping One Tune Recognisable While Its Arrangement Turns Dire: Evidence Review

Yes: if the melody's pitch intervals, contour and rhythm stay fixed, the evidence says listeners will still hear the same tune when the arrangement changes.\[1\] The changes that most reliably add unease (a lower register, roughness or beating, harsh or noisy timbre, unexpected harmony, and hollow textures that use both extreme low and extreme high registers) are mostly in the accompaniment and timbre layers. Those layers are the ones identity studies show matter least for recognising a tune, though timbre still affects recognition of a recently learned tune.

## TL;DR
- **Identity:** Listeners recognise tunes mainly from intervals, contour and rhythm. Adding harmony did not help Kuusi's listeners (N = 46) recognise tunes. Changes of key or tempo stopped hurting recognition after a week, but a change of timbre still hurt it at every delay tested (Schellenberg & Habashi 2015). Keep the lead voice's timbre constant across all three versions and push the change into the accompaniment.
- **Unease:** The best fixed-melody evidence comes from studies that change one musical element at a time. An unexpected chord raised tension ratings (Steinbeis et al. 2006, N = 24). A drone made minor-mode melodies sound sadder (Friedman 2019). Rougher harmonisations conveyed anger and energy across cultures (Athanasopoulos et al. 2021). Lower register made melodies sound more threatening (Huron et al. 2006). In horror-score acoustics, roughness tracks scream-likeness (Trevor et al. 2020), and "anxious" and "terrifying" music differ in texture, dynamics and timbre (Trevor et al. 2023, N = 99).
- **Gaps:** No experiment I found holds a melody fixed while adding a sub-bass layer, synth detuning, a delayed cadence or metric ambiguity and then measures threat. Of those devices, metric irregularity has the weakest support: syncopation did not reliably raise tension ratings. Almost all participants were Western, and much of the harmony evidence depends on familiarity with Western tonality.

## (1) Executive summary (≈150 words)

Studies that pull apart melody, rhythm and harmony consistently find that a tune's identity lives mainly in its pitch intervals, contour and rhythm. Added harmony did not improve recognition (Kuusi 2009).\[2\] After a week, a change of key or tempo no longer hurt recognition of newly learned tunes. A change of timbre, however, still hurt it (Schellenberg & Habashi 2015). Changing the instrumentation can strongly disrupt recognition of orchestral material (Poulin-Charronnat et al. 2004). In practice, you can freely re-harmonise, re-voice, re-register and thin or thicken the accompaniment, but the lead timbre should stay stable. On tension, controlled studies show that the following raise negative or tense ratings: unexpected chords, drones under a minor melody, a lower register, and roughness. Horror-score analyses tie fear to roughness and noise, and they separate anxious music (sparse, extreme registers, quieter, variable loudness) from terrifying music (dense, bright, rough, loud).\[3\] Sub-bass, detuning, delayed cadences and metric ambiguity have not been tested with a fixed melody. Loudness is a major confound in all of these studies.

## (3) Findings by question

### Q1. Does it stay the same tune when only the arrangement changes?

- **[E] Harmony adds little to identity.** In Kuusi's study, 46 Finnish listeners in three expertise groups identified 19 familiar tunes. Melodic versions were easier to recognise than rhythm-only versions, and adding harmony did not help recognition (S1).\[2\]\[36\] Mélen & Deliège found that recognition followed preserved contour and local surface cues rather than the underlying harmonic skeleton (S2).\[5\] Identifying a piece from its chords alone is possible, but it is hard and depends on training and on melodic and rhythmic cues that leak into the chords (S3).\[6\] **Measured identity loss:** S1 and S2 report harmony as non-contributing. Exact percentages were not captured at the access level I had.
- **[E] Key and tempo are weak identity cues; timbre is stronger.** In Schellenberg & Habashi's study, newly learned melodies were transposed by 6 semitones, shifted by 64 bpm, or switched between piano and saxophone (S5). Key and tempo changes reduced recognition at 10 min and 1 day but not at 1 week. A timbre change reduced recognition at every delay, even though listeners had been told to ignore it. The authors conclude that "timbre may be an integral part of the identity of a musical piece," citing Poulin-Charronnat et al. (2004). Changing instrumentation between piano and orchestra hampered recognition of tonal (Liszt) excerpts for musicians and nonmusicians alike\[7\] (S4; timbre-change main effect F(1,65) = 14.56 in one experiment).
- **[E] Without much exposure, listeners group passages by surface features.** On early hearings, similarity judgements follow non-thematic features such as loudness and texture.\[37\] A "theme" dimension that unites variations appears only after repeated listening, and musicians weight it more (S6).\[9\] In free classification, listeners grouped material by tempo, texture, register, contour and articulation, and instrumentation also mattered (S7).\[10\]
- **Cover-version / "same song" studies:** no evidence found. My search on this was not completed (see section 6).
- **[H] Implications for design.** Keep the lead line's intervals, contour, rhythm and timbre identical across "base", "uneasy" and "dire". Make all changes in the accompaniment, register, texture and processing. Do not change the lead synth patch. If you must darken the lead, use filtering or a light detune on the same patch rather than a new instrument. Schellenberg & Habashi cite Lim & Goh (2012), where a change to a similar timbre (violin↔cello) left recognition as good as with the original timbre. S4 and S5 suggest a new lead timbre is the largest identity risk among surface changes. S6 suggests short exposure makes listeners more surface-driven, so first-time players may hear a heavily re-arranged "dire" version as new music unless the melody is prominent in the mix.

### Q2. Does changing the arrangement under a fixed melody change tension or unease?

- **[E] Unexpected harmony raises tension.** Changing one chord in Bach chorales from expected to unexpected to very unexpected raised continuous tension ratings and subjective emotionality in step (N = 24; S8).\[38\]
- **[E] Drones colour emotion, mainly in minor.** A continual tonic drone made minor-mode melodies sound sadder, regardless of whether it sat above or below the melody. It did not make melodies more peaceful (S9).\[12\] When a low drone defined the tonal centre, modes with a minor 3rd above it were rated sadder and more tense (S10).\[12\]\[13\] So a drone mostly makes an already minor-leaning context darker; it does not create unease by itself.
- **[E] Lower register reads as more threatening.** Transposing passages down made them sound more threatening and less submissive (N = 41 music students; S11). Register ranked third among six cues, after mode and tempo, in a factorial design that included "scary" ratings (S12). Caveat: in both studies the whole passage moved, not just the accompaniment.
- **[E] Rougher harmonisations convey negative, high-energy emotion across cultures.** Athanasopoulos et al. kept tempo, timbre, register and melody constant and varied only harmony. A high-roughness harmonisation of simple melodies conveyed energy, dominance and anger to UK, Khow and Kalash listeners alike. Major/minor connotations, by contrast, did not transfer to listeners unfamiliar with Western music (S13).
- **[E, weak] Swapping chords alone barely moves valence.** Two machine-learning studies with small listening tests found that re-harmonising a fixed melody with different chord types "hardly changes" perceived emotion (S14).\[16\] Valence shifted reliably only when key or mode changed (S15).\[39\]
- **[H]** Taken together: under a fixed melody, harmony shifts valence only weakly. Unexpectedness of harmony (S8), roughness (S13), register (S11, S12) and drones in minor (S9, S10) are the better-supported routes to tension. Combining several cues is likely to work better than any single one, because Eerola et al. found the cues act mostly additively, with linear cue levels explaining 77–89% of rating variance (S12).

### Q3. Devices with little testing

- **Sustained drones / pedal tones — [E], limited.** S9 and S10 above. No study I found measured threat or unease (as opposed to sadness or tension) from a non-tonic or dissonant pedal under a fixed melody. Vujović's analysis of pedal points and expectancy is theory only [T] (snippet; https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/231816/360983). \[40\]
- **Sub-bass / very low register — [E], indirect.** Lower pitch reads as more threatening (S11).\[41\] Anxious horror music uses open textures that "lean on" both very low and very high registers (S20; descriptive analysis).\[3\] Inaudible ~18 Hz infrasound under music raised irritability and sadness ratings in a very small study (N = 36; S24).\[31\]\[32\] The 2003 concert result (S25) is press-reported and not peer-reviewed.\[35\] **No study found** that adds an audible sub-bass layer under a fixed melody and measures unease.
- **Detuning / beating / roughness — [E] for roughness, none for detuning itself.** Scream-like horror cues carry more 30–150 Hz amplitude-modulation "roughness" and are rated more negative and arousing (S19).\[22\]\[24\]\[42\] Roughness also drives negative and energetic ratings across cultures (S13), and terrifying excerpts are rougher than anxious ones (S20).\[3\]\[14\] **No evidence found** for a controlled study of synth-voice detune amount and threat. [H] Slow beating from mild detune (a few Hz) is far below the 30–150 Hz roughness band in S19. Expect it to add "unsteadiness" more than alarm, but this is untested.
- **Withheld / deceptive cadences and delayed resolution — [E], partial.** Unexpected chords raise tension (S8).\[43\] In an isolated-cadence study (N = 61), arousal varied little across authentic, deceptive and other cadences. Completion ratings followed the stability of the final chord, and valence followed the preceding mode (S17).\[20\] **No study found** that measures unease as resolution is delayed over a whole loop.
- **Metric ambiguity / irregular pulse — [E], weak or null.** Both anxious and terrifying horror excerpts are rhythmically unpredictable (S20, descriptive).\[3\] Farbood's model includes meter, rhythmic regularity and syncopation as tension parameters, but their separate effects were not visible at abstract level (S16).\[18\]\[44\] Syncopation did **not** reliably change tension and actually raised happiness ratings (S18).\[21\] **No evidence found** that metric ambiguity by itself raises fear or unease.
- **Sparse vs. dense texture — [E], descriptive.** Anxious music is sparse and hollow; terrifying music is dense and massive (S20).\[3\] Note-onset frequency feeds tension in Farbood's model (S16).\[18\]\[45\] [H] This maps neatly onto "uneasy" (thin and hollow) vs. "dire" (dense), but it comes from excerpt analysis, not manipulation.
- **Dynamic / loudness confounds — [E].** Loudness contributes to tension (S16),\[45\] dynamics is a strong emotion cue (S12),\[46\] and terrifying music may be louder than anxious music (S20).\[3\]\[45\] Athanasopoulos et al. had to match loudness to isolate the effect of harmony (S13).\[14\] [H] Loudness-match the three versions (for example to the same integrated LUFS). Otherwise "dire" may read as worse only because it is louder.

### Q4. Cultural and training limits

- **Harmony and mode effects depend on culture [E].** Major/minor connotations did not transfer to Khow and Kalash listeners. Roughness effects did transfer (S13).\[14\] Treat chord-colour and modal darkening (S10, S15, S17) as Western-enculturated effects. Treat roughness (S13, S19) as the most culture-robust cue on the current evidence.
- **Training changes cue use [E].** Professionals use rhythm and harmony cues more in recognition (S1, S3).\[2\] Musicians show stronger timbre-change costs for some material (S4),\[7\] weight thematic identity more (S6),\[9\] and show stronger completion ratings (S17).\[20\] S8 found subjective tension effects in both musicians and nonmusicians.\[11\] S18 tested musicians only.\[21\]
- **Pitch-height and threat [E/T].** S11 is grounded in cross-species "frequency code" theory, but its listeners were Western.\[47\] Cross-cultural replication: no evidence found.
- **Samples.** S8, S12, S19, S20 and S22 used European or North American participants and Western film or classical stimuli. S24 was N = 36.\[31\]

### Q5. Horror and threat score acoustics (primary papers)

- **[E] Trevor, Arnal & Frühholz 2020 (S19).** This was a 2×2 design (voice vs. music × scream-like vs. not). It had 100 vocal recordings from 10 vocalisers plus expert-selected film excerpts, with roughness measured as power in the 30–150 Hz modulation band, followed by valence and arousal ratings.\[22\]\[24\] Scream-like music shares the scream's roughness and its negative, high-arousal ratings, though real screams are stronger.\[23\]\[48\]\[49\]
- **[E] Trevor, Renner & Frühholz 2023 (S20).** In this study, 99 participants rated terror- vs. anxiety-targeted horror excerpts, and the most effective excerpts were then analysed.\[3\]\[50\] This is the most useful single paper for a staged "uneasy → dire" design: anxiety corresponds roughly to "uneasy" and terror to "dire".
- **[E] Blumstein et al. 2010 (S21).** Corpus coding of film soundtracks, with no listener data. Horror soundtracks contain more noise.\[51\]
- **[E] Bullerjahn & Güldenring 1994 (S22).** More than 400 participants, a 10-min film and 5 composed scores. The music changed perceived genre and emotion.\[28\]\[52\] It does **not** isolate acoustic features of threat.
- **[T] Lipscomb & Tolchinsky 2005 (S23).** A theoretical chapter, not an acoustic study of horror.\[53\] It should not be cited as empirical evidence for threat features.

## (4) Where the evidence is thin or absent

- Cover-version or "same song" identity judgements under re-arrangement: no evidence found (search not completed).
- A fixed melody plus an audible sub-bass layer, measuring unease: no evidence found.
- Synth detuning or slow beating (below the roughness range), measuring threat: no evidence found.
- Delayed or withheld resolution across a looping cue, measuring unease: no evidence found. Only single-chord (S8) and isolated-cadence (S17) studies exist.
- Metric ambiguity by itself, measuring fear or unease: no evidence found. Syncopation was null for tension (S18).
- Non-tonic or dissonant pedals, measuring threat: no evidence found.
- Game-specific (interactive, looping) studies of staged arrangement changes: no evidence found.
- Cross-cultural tests of register–threat (S11) and of horror-score features (S19–S21): no evidence found.
- Sample sizes for S2, S3, S5, S7, S9, S10, S12 and S19 were not captured at the access level reached; for S13 only the Kalash sample (N = 34) was captured.

## (5) Five sources a human should read in full (ranked)

1. **Trevor, Renner & Frühholz 2023 (S20).** It is the closest map to "uneasy vs. dire". It lists concrete texture, register, dynamics and timbre contrasts, and it is open access (CC BY) with a stimulus database.\[54\]
2. **Athanasopoulos et al. 2021 (S13).** It is the only fixed-melody harmonisation study that also tests cross-cultural limits and controls loudness. It is open access, and its stimuli are on OSF.
3. **Schellenberg & Habashi 2015 (S5).** It gives the clearest evidence that timbre, unlike key or tempo, stays bound to a melody's identity. This is the single most important design constraint.
4. **Eerola, Friberg & Bresin 2013 (S12).** It is a factorial manipulation of register, timbre, dynamics and mode with "scary" ratings, and it shows the cues add up.\[12\] Use it to plan the size of each change.
5. **Steinbeis, Koelsch & Sloboda 2006 (S8).** It is a clean one-chord manipulation under fixed melodies with tension ratings. It is the best model for designing your own harmony test.
