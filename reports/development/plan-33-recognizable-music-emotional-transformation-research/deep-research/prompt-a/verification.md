## 3. Summary of Contradictions Resolved Across Prompt A Reports

1. **Kuusi (2009) Stimulus Count & Manipulation (`ChatGPT-A` vs. `Claude-A` / `Gemini-A`):**
   - `ChatGPT-A` reported 14 analyzed tunes; `Claude-A` and `Gemini-A` reported 19 tunes. Full-text verification (`argon-f1` and Sonnet PDF extract) confirms **19 familiar tunes were selected and 14 were retained for the primary statistical analysis**.
   - More importantly, `Gemini-A` falsely claimed Kuusi's rhythm+harmony condition featured an "incorrect melodic contour" and conflated Kuusi's findings with reharmonization. In reality, Kuusi **never altered or replaced the original harmony with clashing chords**; Kuusi stripped rhythm (500 ms isochronous tones) in the melody conditions (costing 15–34 points vs. the full tune) and omitted the melody in the rhythm+harmony condition.

2. **Jimenez, Kuusi, & Ojala (2022) Task Inversion (`Gemini-A`):**
   - `Gemini-A` claimed that in Jimenez, Kuusi, & Ojala (2022), *"listeners judged melodies as identical to themselves despite radical harmonic re-accompaniment, largely ignoring background chords in identity judgments."*
   - Both Argon's direct check and `argon-f1` confirmed this is **false**: the vocal melody was fixed across trials and participants ($N = 120$) judged whether the accompanying block chords were the **original song's chords** (Target vs. plausible Lure vs. dissonant Clash). Listeners did *not* ignore the chords—they rated Clash chords near the floor. Direct evidence for "same melody" conservation under reharmonization comes instead from **Liu (2018, 2022)** (`ChatGPT-A`).

3. **Authorship of the 2018 *Music Perception* Drone Paper (`ChatGPT-A` vs. `Claude-A`):**
   - `ChatGPT-A` cited *"Friedman, R., Seror, G., & Neill, W. T. (2018), Tonality Without Structure: Using Drones to Induce Musical Modes and Affect, Music Perception 36(2), 243–249"*.
   - `Claude-A` cited *"Bostwick, J., Seror, G. A., & Neill, W. T. (2018)"* for the exact same DOI (`10.1525/mp.2018.36.2.243`).
   - Verification by Argon and `argon-f2` confirms **J. Jeffrey Bostwick, George A. Seror III, and W. Trammell Neill (2018)** is the true author list; `ChatGPT-A` conflated the first author with Ronald S. Friedman, who authored the follow-up drone paper **Friedman (2019)** in *Psychomusicology* (`10.1037/pmu0000237`) from the same SUNY Albany lab group.

4. **Trevor, Renner, & Frühholz (2023) DOI Conflict (`Claude-A` vs. `Gemini-A`):**
   - `Claude-A` gave DOI `10.1121/10.0016857`; `Gemini-A` gave `10.1121/10.0016847`.
   - Verification (`argon-f2`) confirms **`10.1121/10.0016857`** is the correct DOI (`10.1121/10.0016847` is an unrelated acoustics paper in the same *JASA* volume). However, `Gemini-A`'s extracted descriptive means from the FEARMUS analysis (event density $3.190$ vs. $2.176$, RMS $0.194$ vs. $0.172$, loudness variability $0.561$ vs. $0.513$) were verified as authentic.

5. **Farbood (2012) "65% Loudness Failure" Claim (`Gemini-A`):**
   - `Gemini-A` claimed that in Farbood (2012), *"loudness shifts alone fail to drive perceived tension in 65% of test trials unless paired with pitch/harmonic cues."*
   - Verification (`argon-f2`) refutes this: the 65% statistic referred to stationary-pitch control trials where listeners reported no tension change when pitch was static, *not* a failure of loudness dynamics. Both Farbood (2012) and Granot & Eitan (2011) confirm loudness is a potent tension driver and therefore a major experimental confound that must be normalized when comparing arrangements.

6. **Lipscomb & Tolchinsky (2005) and Hudson & Wang (2025) Classification:**
   - All three reports and `argon-f2` confirm that Lipscomb & Tolchinsky (2005, OUP chapter) and Hudson & Wang (2025, *Music Theory Online* 31.4 on popular-music pedals) are **theoretical/analytical works (`[T]`)** with zero experimental participant data.

## 4. Remaining Evidence Gaps After Prompt A Integration

1. **No parametric "identity-loss threshold" for a newly composed looping game hook:** While **Liu (2018, 2022)** (`[E-abs]`) confirms that adult listeners conserve melody sameness under diatonic and secondary-chord reharmonizations, and **Athanasopoulos et al. (2021)** (`[E-full]`) + **Steinbeis et al. (2006)** (`[E-abs]`) confirm that harmonizations alter perceived affect/tension under a fixed melody, no published study parametrically increases accompaniment dissonance/roughness under a newly learned 8-bar game loop to measure the exact point where "same tune, worse mood" flips into "different tune."
2. **Dissonant/non-tonic pedals under a fixed melody:** **Bostwick et al. (2018)** (`[E-full]`) and **Friedman (2019)** (`[E-abs]`) test modal and tonic drones (showing increased tension/sadness in minor-third modes), but no controlled listener experiment isolates a **dissonant pedal** (e.g., tritone or minor second against the tonic) under an unchanged melody.
3. **Audible sub-bass accompaniment isolated from melody register:** **Huron et al. (2006)** (`[E-full]`), **Granot & Eitan (2011)** (`[E-full]`), and **Eerola et al. (2013)** (`[E-full]`) transposed the *entire passage/melody* downward rather than moving only the bass accompaniment down while holding the lead melody in its original register. Furthermore, classroom laptop/phone speakers roll off below ~100–150 Hz, requiring upper-harmonic saturation (110–240 Hz) to make low bass audible without headphones.
4. **Slow synth detuning (<15 Hz beating) vs. psychoacoustic roughness (30–150 Hz):** **Farbood & Price (2017)** (`[E-full]`), **Pressnitzer et al. (2000)** (`[E-full]`), and **Trevor et al. (2020, 2023)** (`[E-full]`) validate **30–150 Hz psychoacoustic roughness and inharmonicity** as tension/threat cues, whereas slow chorus-like synth pitch drift/beating (1–10 Hz) remains craft-plausible (`[P/H]`) without a dedicated threat-rating study.
