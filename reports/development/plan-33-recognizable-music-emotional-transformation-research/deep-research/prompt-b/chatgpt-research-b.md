# Adaptive Game Music and Theme Transformation: Evidence Review for a Small Authored Browser Game

## Executive summary

The strongest measured evidence supports a narrow conclusion: **game music can measurably change affect, enjoyment, and some forms of engagement, but evidence specifically isolating “adaptive music” from ordinary music remains thin**. [E] Plut & Pasquier’s 2019 within-subject study (final N=30) found that adaptive musical tension increased self-reported player tension relative to static music, while players also rated adaptive soundtracks as matching and adding to the experience; however, ordinary enjoyment measures did not show the same clear inferential result. citeturn198665view0turn198665view2 [E] Klimmt et al. found soundtrack music increased positive affect/enjoyment in *Assassin’s Creed IV* and horror experience in *Alien: Isolation*, but did not establish spatial-presence mediation. citeturn346042search1 [E] A 2026 PLOS ONE review of 14 experiments found game audio modestly improved positively valenced experience versus silence, while the incremental effect of **music over game sound** remained uncertain. citeturn970767view0 [P] Practitioner evidence strongly supports reusable-theme architectures: *Hades*, *Halo*, *NieR: Automata*, *Celeste*, *Dead Space*, and *Genshin Impact* all document controlled variation, layering, sectional substitution, or transformed versions. citeturn565051view0turn126943search0turn565051view4turn565051view2turn126943search3turn486961search0 [H] For a static browser game, the best-evidenced low-cost design is therefore **a small set of recognizably related pre-rendered variants or 2–3 stems, changed at musically safe boundaries when authored story state changes**, rather than a complex generative system.

## Measured effects

### Tension and arousal

[E] **Plut & Pasquier is the cleanest direct adaptive-music comparison located.** Thirty participants completed all four conditions of a within-subject study using the custom Unity game *Galactic Escape*. Participants were aged 17–54 (M=28, SD=10.08), 12 were women, and recruitment mixed students, online game/music communities, Mechanical Turk, and in-person participants. Each participant experienced: no music, static neutral music, adaptive music whose musical tension varied inversely with game tension, and adaptive music whose tension matched game tension. After each roughly 90–120 s run, they completed 13 quantitative and two qualitative items measuring enjoyment-related emotions, experienced valence/arousal/tension, soundtrack matching and soundtrack contribution. citeturn198665view0

[E] The multivariate repeated-measures test was significant, F(39,231)=1.521, p=.032. Experienced tension differed across conditions, F(3,87)=3.662, p=.015, partial η²=.112: mean tension was .55 with no music, .49 static-neutral, .61 inverse-adaptive and .62 congruent-adaptive. Pairwise tests showed neutral→inverse p=.048 and neutral→congruent p=.036. “Soundtrack matching” and “soundtrack experience” produced larger effects, p<.001 in both omnibus tests. citeturn198665view2 The important nuance is that **adaptivity itself appeared more consequential than perfect affective congruence**: both adaptive versions raised experienced tension relative to neutral music, including the deliberately inverted mapping. That makes this study useful evidence that *change contingent on game state* can be perceptually consequential, but it does **not** establish that the audience interprets a worsening arrangement as narrative deterioration. citeturn198665view2

[E] Klimmt et al. tested music presence rather than adaptivity. Study 1 used N=68 young male participants playing an *Assassin’s Creed IV: Black Flag* episode with or without its soundtrack; music increased positive emotional experience and had an indirect effect on enjoyment through positive emotion. Study 2 used N=59 young men playing *Alien: Isolation* with or without soundtrack music; music increased the researchers’ “horror experience” measure, but not general enjoyment. citeturn346042search1 The 2026 systematic review likewise concludes that comparable arousal data are too sparse for meta-analysis and notes that physiological results across studies are mixed. citeturn970767view0turn281238view0

**Implication for your game [H]:** the evidence justifies expecting arrangement changes to alter perceived/experienced tension, but not assuming a simple dose-response relationship such as “more dissonance/layers = proportionally more tension.” Plut & Pasquier explicitly found a non-linear pattern. citeturn198665view2

### Enjoyment, presence and immersion

[E] Heikkilä, Danso & Luck’s 2026 PLOS ONE review is now the strongest high-level synthesis. It screened 3,486 records, included 14 peer-reviewed experimental studies, and meta-analyzed six. Audio-present conditions produced more positively valenced experience than silence, g=.48, 95% CI [.22,.74], with a small-sample-adjusted CI [.14,.82]. One self-selected-music study explained the heterogeneity; excluding it reduced the pooled estimate to g=.34, 95% CI [.18,.50], I²=0%. Critically, the exploratory estimate for **music added over existing game sound** was g=.31 but its adjusted CI crossed zero, [-.07,.68]. citeturn970767view0

[E] That review’s narrative synthesis found frequent positive associations between audio and immersion, flow or motivation, but the engagement constructs were too heterogeneous for pooled analysis. Only two or three studies typically measured any one construct, using instruments ranging from GEQ to presence and transportation scales. citeturn970767view0 This undercuts a common practitioner-level claim that adaptive music reliably “increases immersion”: there are supportive individual studies, but no strong pooled estimate specific to adaptivity. citeturn281238view0

[E] Klimmt et al. are especially useful here because they tested an explicit mediation theory: music was hypothesized to improve enjoyment through affect, spatial presence and character identification. The affective route received support in *Assassin’s Creed IV*; the proposed mediation through spatial presence or identification did not. citeturn346042search1

[E] Hedges, Sazdov & Johnston’s 2024 systematic review similarly concludes that audio elements including music and spatial fidelity generally support immersive experience, while emphasizing inconsistent methods and a threshold beyond which additional audio fidelity may cease to create perceptible benefit. citeturn201823view3

### Noticing transitions

[E] Plut & Pasquier did not directly ask “did you hear a transition?”, but the condition effects on “soundtrack matching” and “soundtrack experience” show that participants distinguished the adaptive states at a perceptual/evaluative level; soundtrack-matching means rose from .40 with no soundtrack context to .48 static-neutral, .53 inverse-adaptive and .63 congruent-adaptive, while “soundtrack experience” rose from .39 to .59, .61 and .75 respectively. citeturn198665view2 These are not transition-detection thresholds.

[E] Hutchings & McCormack, as synthesized in the 2026 PLOS review, found higher immersion and higher perceived correlation between musical and game events with an Adaptive Music System than with a non-adaptive original soundtrack. citeturn281238view0 I could not verify an accessible primary full text with sufficient design details during this search, so it should be treated here as **review-mediated evidence rather than a fully audited primary source**.

[E] McNamara & Arthur directly manipulated transition style: area changes either crossfaded or inserted a short silence. Their accessible Georgia Tech report says few results reached significance and that players in the silence condition had somewhat higher mean Short Flow State Scale scores than crossfade participants. citeturn201823view4 That is useful mainly as a warning against assuming crossfading is intrinsically more immersive; the report does not provide N or enough statistics to support a strong conclusion.

### Repetition fatigue and loop length

[E] The clearest direct manipulation located was McNamara & Arthur: four-area gameplay used either ~15-second loops or ~2.5-minute loops during a 20-minute session. Few results were statistically significant, but players with long loops tended to underestimate elapsed time whereas short-loop participants tended to overestimate it, and qualitative comments were more negative toward short repetition. citeturn201823view4 Because N and inferential details are absent from the open project page, this is weak empirical evidence rather than a loop-length prescription.

[P] Lena Raine independently describes repetition avoidance as a major composition constraint: looped “wallpaper” music may be heard for very long periods, so she deliberately creates enough variation and occasional salient moments to prevent the loop from feeling mechanically repetitive. She also reports repeatedly playing *Celeste* in development to verify transitions and cue alignment. citeturn565051view2

[P] Martin O’Donnell describes the same production concern in *Halo 2*: if an alternate loop persists too long without gameplay progress, the system can fade it rather than continue repeating; he explicitly sought to make loop boundaries inaudible. citeturn126943search0turn126943search2 [P] Darren Korb states that the *Hades* system was designed specifically to extend the “shelf life” of roughly 2.5 hours of music across dozens or potentially hundreds of hours of play by recombining stems and sectional states. citeturn565051view0 These are strong production rationales, but **not measured repetition-tolerance studies**.

## Theme transformation in practice

### Hades

[P] Darren Korb’s *Hades* implementation is unusually well documented. Each biome has a small playlist—four possible Tartarus pieces, three Asphodel pieces and three Elysium pieces. An introductory piece is divided into drum, bass and “everything else” stems; combat activates heavier material and post-combat removes drums. Other biome pieces randomly choose guitar+bass, bass only, or neither. Section markers occur at the beginning of every measure; a state variable allows the piece to advance from a restrained introduction into a fuller folk arrangement, then to a hard-rock section for miniboss/boss encounters, followed by an authored ending tag. citeturn565051view0

[P] Korb later summarizes the dramaturgical architecture explicitly as a progression from “chill” to fuller folk arrangement to hard-rock treatment of the **same piece**, while randomized stems help prevent repeated runs from sounding identical. citeturn565051view1 Identity is therefore preserved through underlying composition and section continuity while arrangement density, instrumentation and energy change. [H] This is extremely close structurally to your desired “one tune, worsening authored state” idea, even though *Hades* ties many changes to combat/progression rather than purely story state.

### NieR: Automata

[P] Keiichi Okabe says that *Automata* deliberately changed the broader sonic palette from the earlier *NieR* while retaining characteristic vocal writing as an identity anchor. citeturn857798search0turn857798search7 [T] PlatinumGames also implemented alternate “8-bit” states for hacking: some tracks had dedicated pre-rendered 8-bit versions; where no special version existed, a real-time filter reduced and distorted the normal mix. The same processing was used to smooth the transition between the normal version and a pre-rendered 8-bit counterpart. citeturn565051view4

[H] For a web game, the transferable lesson is not the real-time spectral processing. It is the **identity-preserving transform**: maintain recognizable structural/thematic material while radically changing timbre, bandwidth, texture or orchestration. This produces a clear change of state without needing a new composition. citeturn565051view4

### Halo

[P] Martin O’Donnell’s *Halo 2* “Quantum Music” system uses authored starts and endings with malleable middle sections. Events can trigger alternate loops; loops are only a few measures long; “glue” material such as sustained pads can bridge between pieces; dramatic hits and drum material can be layered over that substrate; and material can overlap cutscenes rather than abruptly stopping. citeturn126943search0 In a later first-person interview, O’Donnell emphasizes that the goal was for a 2-minute player traversal and a 5-minute traversal both to sound like coherent versions of the same musical story, without audible looping. citeturn126943search2

[P] Identity is preserved less through wholesale arrangement replacement than through recurring thematic material, compatible middles, common harmonic “glue,” and authored end points. [H] For your use case, that suggests a simple alternative to stems: export three variants that share the same form and bar grid, then swap only at safe phrase points.

### Dead Space

[P] Jason Graves delivered *Dead Space 2* cues in four to eight streaming stems. Layers were designed to coexist additively; proximity to tagged threats or locations could continuously raise or lower musical intensity, with combat layers added above suspense layers. citeturn126943search3 In retrospective discussion of the first *Dead Space*, Graves describes four stereo layers simultaneously available in EA’s proprietary system and says each had to function alone as well as combined. citeturn126943search4

[T] This is a high-budget example of **vertical orchestration** rather than theme substitution. [H] The cheap version is simply two or three synchronized files: core theme, deterioration texture, and high-tension layer.

### Celeste

[P] Lena Raine reports personally replaying levels repeatedly to make sure transitions and cues aligned, and describes her loop-writing principle as repetitive enough to establish groove but varied enough to avoid perceived sameness. citeturn565051view2 Her account does not give stem counts or transition durations, so claims that *Celeste* used specific crossfade lengths or exact variant counts would be unverified from the sources I could open.

### Genshin Impact / HOYO-MiX

[P] HOYO-MiX music director Zoe Cai and composer Yu-Peng Chen describe establishing a principal *Genshin Impact* theme and then testing its capacity for transformation. Chen’s development log says an initial bare piano version was used partly to test whether the theme remained listenable without arrangement support and whether it had future variation potential; subsequent iterations added harmony, strings, different lead timbres and a larger orchestral arrangement while retaining the underlying thematic identity. citeturn486961search0

[P] Their broader production model assigns regions distinct musical identities through orchestral foundations plus culturally specific instruments and musical language. citeturn486961search0turn486961search2 This is useful evidence for **theme-identity-through-reorchestration**, but I did not find a first-party HOYO-MiX source documenting exact runtime crossfade durations, stem counts, or a story-worsening sequence in which one theme is progressively degraded.

### Undertale, Journey, DOOM, BioShock Infinite and Silent Hill

[P/T] *Undertale* is widely analyzed for leitmotif reuse, and Toby Fox has publicly discussed reusing and repurposing earlier thematic material, including in official Square Enix conversation material; however, I did not locate an open first-person source in this search that documents the **implementation mechanics or transition policy** needed for your question. citeturn381439search5 Treat specific claims about its runtime transition logic as an **unverified lead**.

[P] Austin Wintory’s GDC description for *Journey* explicitly frames the score as reconciling adaptivity with musicality and constructing large- and small-scale narrative arcs. citeturn857798search4 The GDC Vault page became unavailable during this search, so I could verify the session metadata/overview but not a transcript containing numerical transition details. Exact bar counts or crossfade lengths are therefore **unverified leads**.

[P] Mick Gordon’s GDC session on *DOOM* covers interactive music, but the accessible GDC metadata does not expose implementation numbers. citeturn126943search6 Gordon does describe a deliberate stream of new musical ideas and intensity changes in Bethesda’s first-party interview, and describes reworking an original *DOOM* cue for a modern narrative context. citeturn126943search7 This supports transformation practice but not your exact “same theme worsens with story” mechanic.

[P] Garry Schyman describes game scoring generally as relying on looping, layering and event-triggered intensity; for *BioShock Infinite*, he emphasizes relationship- and story-driven scoring but provides no accessible numerical transition implementation in the source located. citeturn126943search8 I did not find an adequately open first-person Yamaoka source documenting *Silent Hill* theme variants plus implementation mechanics; specific claims about those should remain **unverified leads**.

## Measured versus only claimed

The boundary is important.

**Measured [E].** Adaptive musical state can alter self-reported tension under controlled conditions: Plut & Pasquier provide direct evidence. citeturn198665view2 Music presence can alter positive affect, enjoyment and horror experience, although effects vary by game and outcome: Klimmt et al. provide direct evidence. citeturn346042search1 Across 14 peer-reviewed experiments, game audio has a small-to-moderate positive association with positively valenced experience compared with silence, but the added contribution of music over existing game sound remains uncertain. citeturn970767view0 Evidence for a general immersion effect exists but remains heterogeneous. citeturn201823view3turn970767view0 Evidence that 15-second loops are more fatiguing than 2.5-minute loops is suggestive but weak because the open McNamara/Arthur report provides trends rather than a robust significant effect with complete statistics. citeturn201823view4

**Only claimed or demonstrated in production [P/T].** Korb says randomized stems extend music longevity and help a playthrough feel scored; that is a practitioner rationale, not a listener experiment. citeturn565051view0 Raine says variation reduces the risk of looping music becoming irritating; that is expert practice, not a measured tolerance threshold. citeturn565051view2 O’Donnell says audible loop repetition becomes annoying and designs around it; again, first-person design judgment rather than experimental evidence. citeturn126943search2 HOYO-MiX’s test of whether a bare theme had “variation potential” is a composition-development method, not an experiment measuring player recognition. citeturn486961search0

**Not established by the evidence found [H].** I found no controlled experiment showing that players reliably infer **authored narrative deterioration** from progressively degraded arrangements of a single theme, independent of gameplay performance. I also found no robust empirical rule saying that a transition should occur after X milliseconds, every Y bars, or after a minimum loop length. The existing evidence supports the expressive plausibility of the device, not a validated formula.

## Transition practice and implementation patterns

[T] Middleware documentation distinguishes two broad timing models. FMOD can jump immediately at a transition marker, or quantize transition regions to musical beats/bars; it can also preserve relative position within corresponding bars, even when destination sections differ in tempo. Transition timelines permit source and destination material to overlap and crossfade. citeturn837213search0turn837213search2 Wwise similarly supports explicit source→destination rules, transition segments and musically synchronized switches. citeturn837213search26 These are capabilities, not evidence that one transition style is perceptually superior.

[P] *Hades* gives the clearest shipped numerical synchronization rule: section markers exist at the **start of every measure**, allowing state changes to advance at measure boundaries; drums may switch according to combat state, while larger arrangement changes advance sectionally. citeturn565051view0 Its shipped variant structure also gives concrete scale: 4 biome tracks for Tartarus, 3 for Asphodel and 3 for Elysium, with semi-random stem combinations and multiple energy sections per piece. citeturn565051view0

[P] *Dead Space 2* used **four to eight streaming stems per delivered cue**, layered additively. citeturn126943search3 *Dead Space* itself is described retrospectively as four continuously available stereo musical layers. citeturn126943search4

[P] *Halo 2* used loops “a few measures long,” variable middles, natural authored endpoints and transitional pad material; if a musical loop persisted too long, it could fade instead of repeating indefinitely. citeturn126943search0

[E] The only transition-duration-style experiment located did **not** establish a winning recipe: McNamara & Arthur compared crossfade against a short silence and did not report broad statistically significant superiority for crossfading. citeturn201823view4 Therefore, an exact recommendation such as “use a 500 ms crossfade” would be convention or engineering advice, not evidence-based psychology.

### Cheap enough for a static browser game

[H] The strongest low-budget pattern is **three complete, bar-aligned renders of one theme**: neutral, strained and degraded. Keep tempo, meter, phrase lengths and the theme’s most recognizable contour/rhythm intact; change orchestration, register, density, harmonic support, timbral cleanliness and/or rhythmic stability. Because each file has the same grid, the browser can calculate the next bar or phrase boundary and swap versions with a short equal-power crossfade. This borrows the identity-preserving transformation logic documented in *Hades*, *NieR* and HOYO-MiX without requiring middleware. citeturn565051view1turn565051view4turn486961search0

[H] An equally cheap **two- or three-stem model** uses a persistent “identity” stem plus one deterioration stem and one climax stem. Story state controls gain rather than replacing the whole track. This is a stripped-down analogue of *Hades* and *Dead Space*. citeturn565051view0turn126943search3 For Web Audio, synchronized `AudioBufferSourceNode`s routed through `GainNode`s can do this without FMOD/Wwise; the main engineering requirement is sample-accurate scheduled starts and keeping all variants exported to identical duration/grid.

[H] For authored story deterioration, **phrase-boundary transitions are preferable when the story change can tolerate latency; immediate transitions are preferable when the visual/narrative event itself is abrupt**. That recommendation follows musical continuity practice documented by Korb/O’Donnell and the flexibility provided by FMOD, not controlled perceptual evidence. citeturn565051view0turn126943search0turn837213search2

[H] A very small game probably does not need interactive-music middleware unless you need dozens of transition rules, nested states, live auditioning, platform asset management or elaborate stem randomization. FMOD/Wwise become valuable mainly as authoring/QA tools once the state graph is complex; their transition machinery is far beyond what a three-state Web Audio score requires. citeturn837213search0turn837213search26

A practical three-state design would therefore be: **State A** full recognizable theme; **State B** same grid/theme with reduced warmth or altered orchestration plus one destabilizing layer; **State C** same grid/theme with the strongest timbral/harmonic transformation. Change state at the next phrase point, but allow one authored “hard cut” event late in the story if abrupt rupture is narratively meaningful. [H] This arrangement keeps the experimental variable understandable: the **story state**, not player performance, selects the version.
