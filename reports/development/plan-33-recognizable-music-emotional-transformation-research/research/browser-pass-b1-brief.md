Priority order (stop after ~8 sources or when tool budget is reasonable, about 50 browser actions):

1. **Kuusi (2009), "Tune recognition from melody, rhythm and harmony"** (Univ. of Jyväskylä / ESCOM). Try the JYX repository landing page via search for the title (earlier direct PDF fetch returned 503: https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/handle/123456789/21439/urn_nbn_fi_jyu-2009411339.pdf). *Owner question:* how well can familiar tunes be recognized from melody alone, rhythm alone, melody + harmony, rhythm + harmony — i.e., how much does harmony/accompaniment matter versus melody and rhythm?
2. **Schellenberg & Habashi (2015), "Remembering the melody and timbre, forgetting the key and tempo,"** *Memory & Cognition* 43:1021–1031. Earlier claim: for novel melodies, timbre change lowered recognition at 10 min, 1 day and 1 week; key/tempo changes lowered it at 10 min and 1 day but not 1 week (shifts of 6 semitones and 64 bpm). *Owner question:* N, task, exact shifts, effect sizes.
3. **Schellenberg, Stalinski & Marks (2014), "Memory for surface features of unfamiliar melodies: independent effects of changes in pitch and tempo,"** *Psychological Research* 78:84–95. Earlier claim: key and tempo penalties were additive; listeners were told to ignore the changes.
4. **Dowling & Fujitani (1971), "Contour, interval, and pitch recognition in memory for melodies,"** *JASA* 49(2B):524–531. Earlier claim: for distorted familiar folk tunes, recognizability ranked harmonic-basis-only < contour < contour + relative interval sizes. Verify N and what "harmonic basis only" meant.
5. **Trevor, Arnal & Frühholz (2020), "Terrifying film music mimics alarming acoustic feature of human screams,"** *JASA Express Letters* (find the exact citation; it is believed open access), and, if found, the later Trevor et al. rating study on horror/suspense music acoustics. Earlier claim (from a Scientific American article): terrifying music is loud, brash and dense; anxiety-inducing music uses unexpected sounds and unpredictable rhythm. *Owner question:* which acoustic features (roughness, spectral, tempo, register, density) and what stimuli/sample.
6. **Athanasopoulos, Eerola, Lahdelma & Kaliakatsos-Papakostas (2021), "Harmonic organisation conveys both universal and culture-specific cues for emotional expression in music,"** *PLOS ONE* (open access: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0244964). Earlier claim: same melodies with several harmonizations; the major-happy/minor-sad effect held only for UK listeners (N=101; Kho 34; Kalash 34); roughness correlated only weakly. *Owner question:* what exactly were the harmonization styles, how were stimuli made, which arrangements read as tense/scary/sad, and per-group results.
7. **Eerola, Friberg & Bresin (2013), "Emotional expression in music: contribution, linearity, and additivity of primary musical cues,"** *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:487 (open access, PMC3726864). Earlier claim: 200 synthesized examples, 46 listeners; cue ranking mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, timbre; effects mostly linear/additive. *Owner question:* confirm the cue levels used (e.g., mode, tempo, register), the "scary" results specifically, and the limits.
8. **Steinbeis, Koelsch & Sloboda (2006), "The role of harmonic expectancy violations in musical emotions,"** *J. Cognitive Neuroscience* 18(8):1380–1393. Earlier claim: N=24; one-chord differences in Bach chorales; unexpected chords raised felt tension and EDA. Also **Egermann et al. (2013)** (*Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience* 13:533–553) if time remains.
