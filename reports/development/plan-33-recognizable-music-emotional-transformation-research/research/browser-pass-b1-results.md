## Per-source results

**1. Kuusi (2009), "Tune recognition from melody, rhythm and harmony," ESCOM 2009** — https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_21439 (JYX record; abstract only).
- Design (abstract): 46 participants in three expertise groups (professional, amateur, non-musician); four versions of the first two phrases of familiar tunes (melody, rhythm, melody+harmony, rhythm+harmony); two-factor ANOVA. Stimulus construction and per-cell N not seen.
- Results (abstract, no numbers): version and expertise significant; melodic versions easier than rhythmic regardless of expertise; professionals best, especially on rhythmic versions. Abstract statement: harmony was not found to help tune recognition.
- Verdict: **CANNOT TELL in full**; directionally, melody beats rhythm and adding harmony did not help overall. Familiar tunes only.

**2. Schellenberg & Habashi (2015), *Memory & Cognition* 43:1021–1031** — https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-015-0519-1 (full text).
- Design: 24 unfamiliar folk-song melodies (~30 s) per experiment, each heard twice, then a 7-point old/new recognition rating at 10 min, 1 day or 1 week. Exp 1: N=192 (64 carried over from the 2014 study at 10 min), key shift ±6 semitones or tempo shift ±64 bpm (110 vs 174 bpm). Exp 2: N=96, piano vs alto sax. Listeners were told to ignore the change. Scored as AUC.
- Results: surface-feature main effect F(1,180)=37.03, p<.001, partial η²=.171. Key/tempo cost at 10 min F(1,60)=24.13, p<.001, η²p=.287; at 1 day F(1,60)=21.29, p<.001, η²p=.262; at 1 week F<1 (n.s.). Exp 2 timbre main effect F(1,90)=19.70, p<.001, η²p=.180, no delay interaction. Timbre-change AUC cost .048 at 10 min and .054 at 1 week.
- Limits (authors): unfamiliar Western-tonal melodies only; the authors estimate >400 participants would be needed to detect a small advantage at one week. Worker note: the 1-week null is F<1, not an equivalence test.
- Verdict: timbre cost at all three delays **CONFIRMED**; key/tempo cost at 10 min and 1 day but not 1 week **CONFIRMED** (with the null-result caveat).

**3. Schellenberg, Stalinski & Marks (2014), *Psychological Research* 78:84–95** — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-013-0483-y (abstract + references only).
- Per the 2015 paper's description: Exp 1 used 12 melodies heard twice, ~10 min delay, old/new recognition; half of old melodies shifted by 6 semitones or 64 bpm or both; listeners told to ignore the change; 64 listeners in key and tempo conditions. Abstract: key and tempo effects additive in between-subject (Exp 1) and within-subject (Exp 2) designs.
- Verdict: additive penalties **CONFIRMED** (abstract + 2015 description); own limitations and N not read.

**4. Dowling & Fujitani (1971), *JASA* 49(2B):524–531** — doi 10.1121/1.1912382 (abstract only).
- Design: Exp 1 (2×3 factorial, short-term memory, transposed vs untransposed); Exp 2 (recognition of distorted versions of familiar folk tunes, same length and rhythm). 47 undergraduates in Exp 1 (from a PsycNet snippet); Exp 2 N not seen.
- Results (abstract): Exp 2 ascending recognizability: harmonic basis only < melodic contour < contour plus relative interval sizes (χ²=50.4, p<.001).
- Verdict: ordering **CONFIRMED** from the abstract; pairwise tests, Exp 2 N and the meaning of "harmonic basis" **CANNOT TELL**.

**5. Trevor, Arnal & Frühholz (2020), "Terrifying film music mimics alarming acoustic feature of human screams," *JASA Express Letters* 147(6):EL540–EL545** — doi 10.1121/10.0001459 (full text, open access).
- Design: 200 clips of 800 ms, RMS-normalised: 100 vocal (screams and held "ah") and 100 music (50 scream-like, 50 non-scream-like; 5+5 from each of 10 horror soundtracks from 2010 or later; selection not blind). Roughness = mean modulation power in 30–150 Hz. 20 raters (12 female, ages 21–37) rated valence (−3 to +3) and arousal (1–7).
- Results: roughness higher for scream-like than non-scream-like stimuli (p<.001) in both sound types; screams rougher than scream-like music (M=4.67 vs 4.10, p<.001). Roughness negatively related to valence (music β=−0.196; voice β=−0.443) and positively to arousal (music β=0.07, p=.01; voice β=0.404). Music effects weaker than vocal.
- Limits (authors): non-blind excerpt selection; roughness only; direct manipulation of roughness left to future work. Worker note: loudness was equalised, so not tested.
- Verdict on the earlier claim ("loud, brash, dense; unexpected sounds and unpredictable rhythm"): **PARTLY** — only rough/harsh timbre (roughness) is supported here; loudness, density, rhythmic unpredictability and "anxiety" music were not measured. The later Trevor rating study was not searched.

**6. Athanasopoulos, Eerola, Lahdelma & Kaliakatsos-Papakostas (2021), *PLOS ONE*** — doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0244964 (full text of main article; S1 Appendix stimuli not read).
- Design: two unaccompanied melodies with harmonization variants. Text says eight styles (Bach chorale and Jazz, each major and minor; Organum; Whole-tone; Traditional Greek Epirote; Kalash Drasailak); the statistical model says nine levels, which the worker could not reconcile. Groups: UK 101 (75 female), Kho 34 (all male), Kalash 34 (4 female); authors' power estimate was 37 per group. Ratings: valence, energy, dominance (basic emotions in S5 Appendix, not read). Roughness from a sensory-roughness model.
- Results: major-happy/minor-sad on valence in UK listeners only: major M=3.33 vs minor M=2.89 (t(1180)=3.88, p=.0074); Kho 3.01 vs 3.02 (n.s.); Kalash 2.65 vs 2.83 (n.s.). Roughness vs ratings (Kendall τ): dominance .180 UK / .183 Kalash / .114 Kho; energy .144 / .290 / .148; valence −0.08 UK / −0.105 Kho / **+0.220 Kalash**. Authors: coefficients "rather small". Whole-tone valence: UK 2.13, Kho 2.25, Kalash 4.01. Conclusion: high-roughness harmonization conveys energy, dominance and anger.
- Limits (authors): cross-cultural sampling constraints; no female Kho participants; interpreters used. Worker note: unbalanced groups; Kho and Kalash below the 37-per-group target.
- Verdict: harmonizations of the same melody, UK-only major/minor effect (N=101/34/34), weak roughness correlation all **CONFIRMED**; but the Kalash valence correlation has the **opposite sign**. "Tense"/"scary" ratings are not reported in the main text.

**7. Eerola, Friberg & Bresin (2013), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:487** — PMC3726864 (full text for abstract/methods/results/Table 1; Discussion by keyword only).
- Design: 200 rated examples (D-optimal fractional factorial). Cues: mode (2), tempo (1.2–6 notes/s, 5 levels), dynamics (−10 to +10 dB, 5 levels), articulation (4 levels), timbre (flute, horn, trumpet), register (6 levels, MIDI 53–83), plus four Montreal Battery excerpts as "music structure" (sad, happy, peaceful, fear). Algorithmic generation (Director Musices), sampled instruments. N=46 (20 Stockholm, 26 Jyväskylä; mean age 30.2; musically experienced); four concurrent sliders (happy, sad, peaceful, scary).
- Results: linear cue levels explain 77–89% of variance; quadratic terms add 0–8%; no interactions. Pooled ranking mode > tempo > register > dynamics > articulation > timbre. **Scary (R²adj=.85):** music structure dominates (β=1.56, sr²=.33); register β=−0.23 (sr²=.15; low register = scarier); dynamics β=+0.20 (sr²=.08); mode β=−0.74 (sr²=.08; minor per text); timbre β=0.15 (sr²=.02); tempo β=0.07 (sr²=.01); articulation β=−0.03 (n.s.).
- Limits: one composed excerpt per emotion in the "music structure" factor, so scary-cue effects are conditional on that excerpt; synthetic sampled stimuli; three timbres; highly musical listeners. Authors note the dynamics result may be emotion-specific.
- Verdict: N and design **CONFIRMED**; pooled cue ranking **CONFIRMED only as pooled**; additivity **CONFIRMED**. **CORRECTION for "scary":** music structure dominates, register and dynamics matter, tempo and articulation are negligible.

**8. Steinbeis, Koelsch & Sloboda (2006), *J. Cognitive Neuroscience* 18(8):1380–1393** — doi 10.1162/jocn.2006.18.8.1380 (abstract only via PubMed 16859422).
- Design (abstract): N=24 (12 musicians, 12 non-musicians); six Bach chorales in three versions differing in one chord (expected, unexpected, very unexpected); subjective tension and emotionality, EDA, heart rate, EEG.
- Results (abstract): tension, overall emotionality and EDA increased with harmonic unexpectedness; larger early negativity for very unexpected chords; musicians larger P3. No effect sizes in the abstract.
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED** from the abstract; statistics **CANNOT TELL**.

## Worker's closing summary

Top corrections: (1) Eerola 2013 ranking is pooled — for "scary", music structure dominates, register and dynamics matter, tempo/articulation negligible. (2) Trevor 2020 tested roughness only; reword. (3) Athanasopoulos 2021: eight vs nine harmonization levels unreconciled; Kalash roughness–valence correlation positive (+0.22); Kho and Kalash groups below the authors' own target. Plain-language: melody carries tune identity, and harmony did not help recognition (Kuusi, abstract); timbre change hurts recognition of unfamiliar melodies at all tested delays, key/tempo at 10 min and 1 day only; roughness, low register, louder dynamics and minor mode are the most reliable "scary" cues in these studies; the major-happy/minor-sad effect was UK-only in the 3-group sample; unexpected chords raise felt tension and skin conductance (abstract only).
