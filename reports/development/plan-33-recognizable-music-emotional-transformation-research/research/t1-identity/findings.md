## 1. Key findings

1. **[E] Contour and exact intervals both carry identity, and they separate under transposition.** With transposed melodies, listeners could not reliably tell a melody from a same-contour variant, while same-vs-different judgments stayed easier (S1, abstract). Dowling's 1978 theory treats contour as a memory component separate from scale (S2, snippet).
2. **[E] Distorted familiar folk tunes (original length and rhythm kept):** recognizability ranked harmonic basis only < contour < contour plus relative interval sizes (S1). The nearest thing here to a robustness ranking; a single 1971 study on folk tunes.
3. **[E] For familiar tunes in long-term memory, pitch structure was more recognizable than rhythm**; the best cue combined pitch and duration (S3, snippet).
4. **[E] Note-by-note identification of familiar tunes** was predicted by phrase-edge notes, notes completing a rise-and-fall, and metrically accented notes (S14, snippet).
5. **[E] Timbre is the most durable surface cue.** For novel melodies, a timbre change lowered recognition at 10 min, 1 day and 1 week; key and tempo changes lowered it at 10 min and 1 day but not at 1 week (S7, abstract). Halpern & Müllensiefen report timbre and tempo change both impair explicit memory (S4, snippet; year conflict 2007/2008).
6. **[E] For unfamiliar melodies heard twice, key and tempo penalties were additive, not interacting** (S8, abstract). Shifts in S7: 6 semitones, 64 bpm.
7. **[E] Familiar tunes retain absolute key and tempo.** Untrained adults identified the original key of familiar TV-theme excerpts shifted 1–2 semitones; unfamiliar controls were at chance (S9, abstract). Singers reproduced familiar songs' tempo within 8% on 72% of productions (S6, abstract; 46 participants; a singing task).
8. **[E] Familiar songs drift less toward a tempo context** than unfamiliar songs (S21, snippet).
9. **[E] Novel melodies were retained after two exposures with no decline over a week** (S7). Recognition held across 1 to nearly 200 intervening melodies (S20, snippet). Structural features predicted novel-melody memory only weakly, r ≈ .05–.06 (S12, snippet).
10. **[E] Short excerpts carry identity.** 200 ms clips of recorded popular songs were identified above chance; 100 ms needed dynamic high-frequency information; backward playback dropped to chance (S5, snippet). 400 ms clips were identified on more than 25% of trials for title and artist (S13, snippet).
11. **[E] Mode and timbre interact.** Congruent pairs were faster in a reaction-time task; incongruent pairs were later recognized better (framed as desirable difficulty) (S25, abstract; N not captured).
12. **[E] Infants (8–11 months, N=177)** judged contour-preserving transformations better than transpositions (S11, snippet). Adults remembered own-culture novel excerpts better regardless of expertise (N=150, US and Turkey; S19, snippet).
13. **[E, correlational] Frequently named earworms** had more typical global contours and faster average tempi than matched non-earworm songs (S22, snippet).
14. **[T] Cover-song (version-identification) systems treat key and tempo as nuisance dimensions** (transposition search or key estimation; beat-synchronous or alignment tempo handling). Key-estimation errors propagate and can worsen retrieval (S15, S16, snippets). These are machine systems, not listener evidence.
15. **[T/P] Leitmotif scholarship** frames the device as thematic plus associative, with thematic development and identity as a theme-level topic (S23, publisher description). Reference sources describe transformation as a dramatic function (S24, lead).
16. **[H] No verified study the worker read isolates how changing only the chord accompaniment affects tune identity.** Kuusi 2009 (S27) and a 2022 harmony-memory study (S28) are the closest leads; their results were not read.

## 2. Uncertainty and context limits

- Most identity evidence uses **familiar** songs (S3, S5, S9, S13, S14). Novel-hook evidence is almost entirely old/new surface-memory tasks (S4, S7, S8, S12, S20). Extrapolating to a new hook is inference.
- Old/new memory is not the same as "same tune?" across arrangements. In S8 listeners were told to ignore key and tempo changes, so measured costs may answer a different question.
- Apparent conflict: familiar tunes keep absolute key/tempo (S6, S9), but novel melodies show short-delay key/tempo penalties (S7). Familiarity may reconcile this; no source read tests it directly.
- Samples are mostly Western college students; musician vs non-musician effects are not settled in what was readable.
- Sample sizes and effect sizes are missing for most studies, including S1 and S25.
- No source read tests a gradual morph between arrangements.

## 3. Transferable recommendations

- **R1 [E, med]** Keep the hook's exact interval sequence and contour identical across calm, uneasy and dire versions (S1, S2, S3).
- **R2 [E/H, med-low]** Keep rhythm skeleton, loop length and phrase-end/downbeat notes fixed (S14, S3); vary surrounding rhythm (accompaniment, ornament) before the hook's onsets.
- **R3 [E, med]** Treat timbre change as a real identity cost; recognition loss persisted at one week (S7, S4, S18). Change accompaniment and texture before swapping the lead voice.
- **R4 [E, med-low]** Avoid changing key, tempo and timbre in the same step; penalties add (S8). Shift one anchor at a time; keep shifts modest.
- **R5 [H, low]** Once players know the base version, key or tempo shifts may become easier to hear (S9, S6), though context pulls tempo judgments less for familiar songs (S21). Test whether later arrangements need smaller shifts.
- **R6 [H, low]** Accompaniment-only change with melody and rhythm fixed is a plausible identity-preserving move; S1 suggests harmony alone is the weakest identity carrier among the distortions tested. Treat as a variable to test, not as safe.
- **R7 [H, med]** Paired-listening design: change one anchor per pair (timbre; key by 1–2 semitones or more; tempo step such as S7's 64 bpm or smaller; accompaniment only; rhythm; contour as a foil). Use same-vs-different with a same-contour foil (Dowling-style, S1) plus old/new recognition at 10 min and 1 day (S7). Report N, musical training and exact instructions. Measures recognition only, not emotional or addictive effects.

## 4. Open questions for the owner (from worker)

4. Musicians or non-musicians as audience? Enculturation and training effects are mixed (S19, S25).
