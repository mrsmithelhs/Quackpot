## 1. Key findings

1. **[E] Mode is one cue, not always the strongest.** Eerola, Friberg & Bresin 2013 (200 synthesized examples, 46 listeners, four emotion labels) ranked cues as mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, timbre; the order varied by emotion, and dynamics mattered more for "scary" and "peaceful". [S1]
2. **[E] Cue effects were mostly linear and close to additive** (77–89% of variance; no interactions after correction). Harmony, contour and dissonance were held constant in that study, so it does not directly test arrangement-level tension. [S1]
3. **[E] An unexpected chord raises felt tension on a fixed melody.** Steinbeis et al. 2006 (N=24; one-chord differences in Bach chorales; tension and electrodermal activity rose; snippet only). Egermann et al. 2013 (N=50, live flute; expectation violations went with arousal; abstract only). [S9, S2]
4. **[E] Chord tension depends on tonal function, sensory consonance, horizontal motion and training** (Bigand, Parncutt & Lerdahl 1996; abstract only). [S7]
5. **[E] Register and interval character.** Costa et al. 2000 (N=43; search summary): low-register intervals were rated more negative, and dissonant intervals more negative, unstable and tense. Pallesen et al. 2005 (fMRI; snippet): minor and dissonant chords produced more activity than major in passive listening. Neural activity is not felt tension. [S11, S10]
6. **[E] Same melody, different harmonizations.** Athanasopoulos, Eerola et al. 2021 (PLOS ONE, full text; UK N=101, Kho N=34, Kalash N=34): the major-happy/minor-sad effect held only for UK listeners; roughness correlated weakly with emotion, with valence direction differing by group. This is the closest study to the owner's design. [S6]
7. **[E] Culture and dissonance.** McDermott et al. 2016 (partial; PDF captions only): Tsimane' listeners rated consonant and dissonant chords as equally pleasant, whereas Bolivian town and city residents preferred consonance. Bowling et al. 2018 (N=30, full text) argues consonance tracks vocal similarity; a published critique (Goffinet; snippet only) disputes the tuning. The consonance literature is contested. [S3, S8]
8. **[E] Texture interacts with mode and tempo.** Webster & Weir 2005 (N=177; snippet): major, non-harmonized and faster stimuli rated happier; interactions were reported but not read. [S13]
9. **[E] Loudness and tempo raise tension.** Ilie & Thompson 2006 (search summary). Farbood & Upham 2013 (one recorded song; analysis, not manipulation): loudness acted immediately, harmony over a longer window; legato and a held fermata sustained tension. Krumhansl 1997 (N=40; snippet): tension correlated most with fear. [S17, S5, S20]
10. **[E] Mode and tempo each drive happy–sad**, tempo more salient (Gagnon & Peretz 2003; abstract). Juslin 1997 (snippet): listeners use synthesized cues probabilistically and cue weight varies by emotion. [S12, S15]
11. **[E, lead only] Horror/suspense.** A secondary account (Scientific American) of work by Trevor and colleagues (JASA; **primary not opened**) says terrifying music is loud, brash and dense, while anxiety-inducing music uses unexpected sounds and unpredictable rhythm. A 2022 suspense study (snippet, login-gated) reports tense music alone induced suspense. [S19, S23]
12. **[T] Expectation is a plausible mechanism** (Juslin & Västfjäll 2008; Huron 2006). **No empirical test of delayed or withheld resolution was found.** [S16, S18]
13. **[T/E] Tension is temporal and multidimensional** (Farbood 2012, abstract; Lerdahl & Krumhansl 2007, snippet). [S21, S14]
14. **[P] Herrmann's *Psycho* score** is for string orchestra, described as a "black and white sound" (LA Phil page, secondary; no composer interview found). [S22]

## 2. Uncertainty and context limits

- Stimuli are short, synthesized or isolated; most samples are Western and often trained.
- Loudness and tempo confound tension ratings; genre conventions shape what sounds "dire".
- Physiology (EDA, fMRI) is not the same as felt tension.
- Ilie & Thompson's pitch-height results conflict between secondary sources, so they are not used.
- The consonance/dissonance literature is contested (McDermott vs Bowling vs critique).
- No study compares subtle and clear shifts directly.

## 3. Technique list (technique → impression; evidence; strength)

- Unexpected chord at a phrase point → more tension; [E] (F3); medium.
- Minor/modal mixture → sadder, not necessarily dire; [E] (F1, F6, F10); context- and culture-dependent.
- Dissonant/tritone voicings → tension; [E] (F4, F5); low–medium.
- Pedal/drone, sustained non-resolving harmony → sustained tension; [T] plus [E] (held fermata); low, **untested**.
- Register lowering → more negative; [E] (F1, F5); medium for intervals, low for whole melodies.
- Louder → more tense; [E] (F9); medium, **must be level-matched**.
- Faster → more tense; [E] (F9, F10); medium, confounded.
- Texture/density → shifts happy–sad, interacts with mode; [E] (F8); low–medium.
- Articulation/timbre → weaker than mode/tempo/register; [E] (F1); low.
- Rhythm/syncopation/meter → tension; [T] (F13); low.
- Withheld cadence → expectancy; [T] (F12); low, untested.
- Dense loud texture → terror; [E lead] (F11); low.

**Subtle vs clear [H]:** *Subtle:* one unexpected chord at a cadence; a held or unresolved phrase end; a level-matched modest loudness or tempo step; a small bass lowering. *Clear/dire:* stacking several cues in the same direction (additivity from F2, but stacking not tested); dense loud texture (lead only); low register plus dissonant intervals.

## 4. Recommendations for a later paired-listening test (not clinical)

- Same melody, tempo and timbre; **loudness matched** (LUFS/RMS plus listener level check); randomized and counterbalanced.
- Three arrangements varying one parameter group each: (A) harmony/voicing only (one unexpected chord at one cadence, bass fixed); (B) bass/register only; (C) density or rhythm only. Then one stacked version to test additivity.
- Ratings: continuous tension slider, forced-choice tense/uneasy/dire, and a "still the same tune?" recognition item. Record listener training/background.
- R1 [E, med]: harmony at one or two cadences is the first lever (F3, F4).
- R2 [E/T, med]: do not rely on mode alone (F1, F2).
- R3 [E, med]: level-match everything (F9).
- R4 [E, low]: test register and bass separately (F5).
- R5 [H, low]: test a withheld resolution (F12).
- R6 [H, low]: drone, sub-bass and detuning are untested; pilot them first.
- R7 [E, low]: treat texture as a separate pilot (F8).
