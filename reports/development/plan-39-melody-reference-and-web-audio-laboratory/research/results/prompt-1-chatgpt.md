# Extending “Lucky Ripple”: Phrase Continuation, Voice Leading, and Loop Architecture for an Octave-Spanning Tonal Game Hook

## Structural diagnosis and design thesis

Motif C is unusually self-sufficient for a two-bar game hook. Its first bar outlines tonic through scale degrees \(\hat3\)-\(\hat5\)-\(\hat1\), briefly leans downward through \(\hat7\), and returns through tonic-chord tones; Bar 2 introduces \(\hat6\), then descends \(\hat5\)-\(\hat3\)-\(\hat1\), with the final D5 occupying the last two beats. In tonal-perceptual terms, that combination of tonic arpeggiation, descending closure, and a metrically long final tonic strongly reinforces D as a stable endpoint. Research on tonal perception consistently finds that listeners organize pitches hierarchically around a tonal center and hear tonic-related tones as especially stable; tonal context also improves pitch processing and expectancy. citeturn544936search10turn544936search6

The important consequence is that **the problem is not really “how do I compose six more bars?”** It is “how do I make a completed two-bar utterance function as the basic idea of a larger form?” Classical phrase theory offers exactly that distinction. In Caplin’s model, an eight-measure sentence does not simply continue adding melody: it presents an idea, destabilizes or fragments it, and then drives toward a cadence; a period instead places related opening material into antecedent and consequent phrases with weaker and stronger endings. citeturn893769search9turn254289search1

For this particular hook, the strongest solution is a **hybrid period/sentence architecture**:

| Bars | Formal role | What the listener should hear |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Basic idea / hook statement | Motif C essentially intact |
| 3–4 | Response + continuation | Small fragments of C, harmonic activation, open ending on V |
| 5–6 | Consequent return | Motif C unmistakably returns, lightly reharmonized |
| 7–8 | Cadential continuation + turnaround | One compressed hook fragment, then a dominant-colored seam into Bar 1 |

This structure solves all three failed-extension problems at once. It avoids a static tonic field because harmony and accompaniment change; it avoids upper-register filler because Bars 3–4 are derived from recognizable fragments rather than a second melody; and it guarantees the return of the hook in Bars 5–6. The logic closely matches classical antecedent–consequent practice, where a weaker first ending is followed by a stronger or more definitive second phrase, while also borrowing the sentence’s use of fragmentation and increasing activity in the continuation. citeturn893769search0turn893769search2

That approach is also consistent with game-composer practice. Koji Kondo has explicitly associated successful repetition with having several distinct motifs rather than mechanically reiterating one idea, and he emphasizes memorable melody and rhythm as central to game music. citeturn501215view0turn550548view0 Lena Raine has described beginning *Celeste* from a memorable central melody while using motifs and ambience to keep game scoring from feeling excessively repetitive. citeturn254289search4 Yu-Peng Chen similarly describes keeping exploration themes and motives simple, varying orchestration around recurring themes, and thinking polyphonically about interaction among melodic lines. citeturn254289search5

The key compositional principle, therefore, is **identity in the foreground, novelty underneath it**. Do not ask the melody to provide all eight bars of novelty. Let the hook remain extremely recognizable while harmony, bass, register, instrumentation, inner voices, articulation, and rhythmic density create the larger-scale motion.

## Period, sentence, and accompaniment-led models

A conventional period is especially useful when the main motive already sounds syntactically complete. Classical periods typically consist of antecedent and consequent phrases; the antecedent generally closes more weakly—often with a half cadence—while the consequent returns to related opening material and ends more strongly. citeturn893769search0turn893769search1 Your obstacle is that Motif C itself already behaves more like the *consequent* of a period than an antecedent: its D5 ending is too settled.

The way to recover periodic behavior is not to rewrite Bars 1–2 drastically, but to make **Bars 3–4 retroactively reinterpret Bars 1–2 as only the first half of a larger thought**. Bar 3 should reactivate motion immediately after D5, while Bar 4 should end on A or A7 rather than D. The listener then experiences Bars 1–4 as “closed locally, open globally.” Classical formal theory readily allows such larger-scale reinterpretation: local cadential gestures do not necessarily terminate the larger formal process, and post-cadential or continuation material can extend beyond a local arrival. citeturn893769search18turn893769search6

A sentence gives a different solution. Caplin’s archetypal sentence is an eight-measure theme whose first half functions as presentation and whose second half combines continuation and cadential function. citeturn893769search9 The continuation typically creates greater instability through fragmentation, harmonic motion, or more compressed motivic activity; later analytical work continues to identify repetition of a basic idea followed by fragmentation as a defining sentential behavior. citeturn893769search14turn893769search2

For Lucky Ripple, a strict sentence—C, C again, then four bars of continuation—would probably overexpose the hook too quickly. A better adaptation is **C → fragment/answer → C′ → cadence**. That preserves the sentence’s trajectory without demanding two literal presentations in the first four measures.

The third useful model is accompaniment-led call-and-response. This is particularly appropriate for cozy game music because “response” need not mean another lead melody. It can be a bass figure, pizzicato answer, mallet echo, celesta dyad, or rhythmic ostinato. Kondo’s comments on the *Super Mario Bros.* overworld theme are revealing here: he identifies contrasting rhythmic subdivisions between melody/bass and hi-hat as a source of forward movement, and attributes the music’s resistance to monotony partly to having multiple distinct motifs. citeturn550548view0 Chen likewise describes composing polyphonically and considering how melodic lines interact, while using different orchestrations of the same theme to change atmosphere. citeturn254289search5

For this cue, the three models can be ranked practically:

| Model | Suitability | Main advantage | Main risk |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Hybrid period | **Excellent** | Guarantees recognizable return and clear 8-bar shape | Bar 4 must remain genuinely open |
| Adapted sentence | **Excellent** | Creates propulsion without inventing a second tune | Too much fragmentation can become busy |
| Accompaniment-led response | **Essential as a layer** | Fills silence without competing with hook | Too melodic a countermelody recreates the “filler” failure |

The recommendation is not to choose only one. Use **periodic large-scale organization, sentential continuation techniques, and accompaniment-led local responses**.

## Repairing the Beat 6–8 vacuum

Strictly speaking, Beats 6–8 are not silence: D5 is sounding continuously. The perceptual vacuum comes from the absence of **new attacks** in the lead. That distinction matters. The sustained tonic should remain; what changes is the activity around it.

The worst solution is to add another high-register melody over D5. The best solution is to let the sustained D function as a **pedal-like common tone while the harmony underneath changes function**. Tonal research shows that melodic identity can remain perceptually stable across changes in harmonic context, particularly when the reharmonization remains diatonic. citeturn544936search3turn544936search1

A particularly effective two-beat bridge is:

| Bar 2 location | Lead | Bass | Inner/answer voice | Harmony heard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beat 3 | D5 half-note continues | B2 quarter | F#4–A4 eighths | Bm7 / vi7 |
| Beat 4 | D5 continues | G2 eighth → A2 eighth/quarter | B4–A4 eighths | G(add6) → A7sus4 |
| Bar 3 beat 1 | new phrase begins | A2 or D3 depending design | C#4 resolves if using dominant | V continuation or new predominant |

The crucial move is **D5 over A harmony at the end of the bar**. D becomes scale degree 4 over the dominant, so a pitch that previously sounded like complete tonic closure is recontextualized as a suspension-like tension. You do not need to alter the melody note itself to reopen the phrase. This is an analytical/compositional inference from tonal function rather than a direct experimental result, but it follows the general finding that perceived melodic tones are strongly shaped by their harmonic context. citeturn544936search7turn544936search9

If A7sus4 feels too tense for a cozy opening environment, soften the dominant by omitting G and treating the final sonority as **A(add4)/E** or **A5(addD)**. The important psychological effect is simply that the bass has left D.

A gentler version is a **bass hocket**:

`Bar 2 beat 3: B2 (quarter) | beat 3&: rest | beat 4: G2 (eighth) | beat 4&: A2 (eighth)`

Against the sustained D5, the bass changes its intervallic meaning from minor third above B, to fifth above G, to fourth above A. The listener hears continued motion although the foreground tune is resting.

A more playful answer can use a small fragment lifted directly from C rather than a novel countermelody:

`F#4–A4–B4–A4`, all eighth notes across Beats 3–4.

This reuses \(\hat3-\hat5-\hat6-\hat5\), pitches already salient in the hook. Put it at least an octave below the hook’s highest point and at reduced velocity—approximately 60–75% of the lead’s perceived level—so it reads as an echo, not a second protagonist. That recommendation is stylistic rather than experimental, but it is consistent with the broader game-music practice of preserving recognizable motifs while changing texture and orchestration. citeturn254289search4turn254289search5

A third method is **rhythmic hand-off**. When the D5 begins on Bar 2 Beat 3, introduce a two-beat percussion or plucked ostinato:

`Beat 3: x . x . | Beat 4: x . x x`

where the final eighth attack anticipates the next bar. Because metrical perception is itself hierarchically organized, rhythmic events can supply forward expectation even while pitch remains stationary. Modern research treats tonal and metrical organization as core, partly independent perceptual systems in music. citeturn544936search10

The general orchestration rule is therefore:

**When the hook stops articulating, transfer activity downward or sideways rather than upward.**

That means bass motion, inner-voice fragments, percussion, arpeggiated chord tones, or timbral changes—not a new soprano tune.

## Diatonic reharmonization and melody conservation

The most directly relevant empirical work is Jing Liu’s research on “melody conservation.” In the 2018 study, adult listeners judged whether melodies remained the same under changes of harmonic context. Participants were significantly better at conserving melody when harmony changed from primary chords to **other diatonic chords** than when it changed to secondary/chromatic harmony. citeturn544936search3 Liu’s 2022 cross-cultural replication/extension found that Chinese listeners were also able to recognize melodic sameness across altered harmonizations, supporting the broader conclusion that melodic identity can survive considerable harmonic change. citeturn544936search0turn544936search1

This evidence justifies keeping Bars 3–6 almost entirely within D major if recognizability is the priority. It does **not**, however, experimentally prove that vi is superior to iii or ii. Liu grouped harmonic treatments categorically; the studies do not provide a rank order of D-major ii, iii, and vi for your exact melody. Any such ranking must therefore come from chord-tone compatibility and tonal-function analysis rather than from Liu directly. citeturn544936search3turn544936search1

For Lucky Ripple, duration-weighted chord-tone compatibility produces a useful result. Across the eight beats of Motif C:

- D occupies 3.0 beats.
- F# occupies 1.5 beats.
- A occupies 1.5 beats.
- B occupies 1.0 beat.
- C# occupies 1.0 beat.

Against **vi7 = B–D–F#–A**, 7 of the motif’s 8 beats are chord tones; only C# falls outside the chord. Against **iii = F#–A–C#**, approximately 4 beats are chord tones. Against **ii7 = E–G–B–D**, approximately 4 beats are chord tones, concentrated heavily at the B and D end of the phrase. These values are direct calculations from the supplied melody rather than claims from the literature.

That makes **vi7 the best color substitution for preserving the literal hook**, but not necessarily the best harmony for creating forward motion. ii has strong predominant utility in common-practice tonal syntax, while vi often behaves more like a tonic substitute or tonic-prolongational harmony. Caplin’s formal theory emphasizes that phrase functions are closely tied to characteristic local harmonic progressions, especially the movement toward predominant and dominant areas in cadential spans. citeturn893769search10turn893769search21

Accordingly, use the chords for different jobs:

| Chord | Best job in this cue | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **vi / vi7** | Recoloring a returned Motif C | Extremely high pitch compatibility with the hook |
| **ii / ii7** | Bars 3–4 continuation | Creates clear movement away from tonic toward V |
| **iii / iii7** | Brief passing color | Shares F#, A, C# with the opening but is less functionally decisive |
| **IV / IV6** | Warm predominant / pastoral color | Strong for cozy texture and smooth bass motion |
| **V / V7 / Vsus** | Bar 4 and Bar 8 openings | Creates the incompleteness needed for continuation or looping |

For a beginning-of-game space, a good harmonic rhythm is **one or two chords per bar, not continuous eighth-note changes**. The lead is already rhythmically distinctive; harmonic hyperactivity would compete with it. Chen’s description of exploration scoring emphasizes simple motives, soothing pacing, and restraint rather than continuous stimulation. citeturn254289search5

A highly usable progression is:

**Bars 1–8:**  
`D | Bm7 – A(sus) | Gmaj7 | Em7 – A7 | Bm7 | Gmaj7 – D/F# | Em7 | A7sus4 – A`

This is deliberately not a “songwriter loop” that resolves every two bars. Bars 1–2 preserve the original tonic arrival; Bars 3–4 reinterpret it as part of a larger trajectory; Bars 5–6 provide the recognizable return over fresh harmonic color; Bars 7–8 point back toward Bar 1.

If you want an even softer palette, use:

`D(add9) | Bm7 – G/A | G6 | Em7 – A(add4) | Bm7 | D/F# – G6 | Em7 | Asus4`

Here the dominant is softened by suspended tones and omitted leading-tone emphasis until the seam.

## A concrete eight-bar composition

The following design is the strongest match for your brief because it retains Motif C twice, gives Bars 3–4 a clear derived identity, and makes Bar 8 function as a pickup into Bar 1 rather than a second terminal cadence.

### Bars 1–2: hook statement

Keep Motif C **exactly as written**. Do not “fix” its closure yet.

Recommended accompaniment:

`Bar 1: D(add9)`  
Bass: D3 on Beat 1, A2 on Beat 3.  
Inner pulse: light eighth-note D4–A4 or F#4–A4 dyads on offbeats.

`Bar 2: Bm7 → G/A or A7sus4`  
Beat 1 bass B2.  
Beat 3 bass G2.  
Beat 4 bass A2.

The melody’s final D remains unchanged. The bass/harmony supplies the reopening.

### Bars 3–4: derived response, not new melody

Use only fragments that the listener can trace back to the hook.

A recommended Bar 3 lead:

`Beat 1: F#5 eighth`  
`Beat 1&: A5 eighth`  
`Beat 2: B5 quarter`  
`Beat 3: A5 eighth`  
`Beat 3&: F#5 eighth`  
`Beat 4: E5 quarter`

This is recognizably built from the hook’s F#–A gesture and B–A–F# descent, but E5 is new enough to signal continuation.

Harmony: `Gmaj7 | Em7`

A recommended Bar 4 lead:

`Beat 1: F#5 quarter`  
`Beat 2: E5 eighth`  
`Beat 2&: F#5 eighth`  
`Beat 3: A5 quarter`  
`Beat 4: C#6 quarter`

Harmony: `Em7 | A7`

Ending Bar 4 on C#6 over A7 is ideal because C# is the third of V and also a pitch already present prominently in Motif C. The phrase is therefore open without sounding as though a foreign theme has arrived. Classical-period theory predicts exactly this kind of weak/open first-half ending before a consequent return. citeturn893769search0turn893769search1

If C#6 feels too bright, end on A5 instead. That produces an even clearer dominant arrival.

### Bars 5–6: recognizable return with harmonic refresh

Replay Motif C almost literally. The safest change is **harmonic**, not melodic.

Suggested harmony:

`Bar 5: Bm7 → D/F#`  
`Bar 6: Gmaj7 → D/F# or Em7`

Because Bm7 contains B, D, F#, and A, nearly the entire hook fits it as chord tones. The listener therefore hears “the same tune in different light” rather than “a variation that needs relearning.” The perceptual plausibility of preserving melodic identity under diatonic reharmonization is supported by Liu’s studies, although the specific Bm7 recommendation is an analysis of this motif rather than an experimentally tested preference. citeturn544936search3turn544936search1

For additional freshness, change instrumentation instead of pitch. For example:

Bars 1–2: celesta/pluck lead.  
Bars 5–6: same lead doubled quietly one octave below by clarinet, soft synth, or marimba.

Chen specifically describes reusing the same thematic material under different orchestration to establish different atmospheres, while Raine’s comments similarly emphasize memorable thematic identity combined with changing context. citeturn254289search5turn254289search4

### Bars 7–8: compressed cadence and loop pickup

Do **not** compose a third full tune.

Bar 7 should fragment the hook:

`F#5–A5` eighths,  
`D6` quarter,  
rest quarter,  
`A5–F#5` eighths.

That gives the ear one last unmistakable reference without reproducing the entire two-bar gesture.

Harmony: `Em7` moving toward `A`.

Bar 8 should be principally accompaniment-led. A particularly effective design is:

Lead:  
Beat 1 A5 quarter  
Beat 2 F#5 quarter  
Beat 3 rest  
Beat 4 rest

Bass:  
Beat 1 E3  
Beat 2 A2  
Beat 3 A2  
Beat 4 C#3 eighth → E3 eighth

Upper accompaniment on Beat 4: `A4–C#5–E5` or a sparse C#5–E5 dyad.

Harmony: `Em7 | A7`

Then Bar 1 begins F#5–A5–D6 over D.

The seam therefore contains a clear **V → I** harmonic arrival, but the melodic protagonist enters *after* the turnaround rather than completing a cadence before the loop point. That distinction is essential: Bar 8 should feel like inhalation; Bar 1 supplies the exhalation and arrival.

For a still gentler seam, use `A7sus4 → A` with the suspension resolving in a lower inner voice while the lead rests.

The resulting formal shape is:

**A — a — A′ — turnaround**  
or, in classical-functional language, approximately  
**basic idea / response — continuation to HC — consequent return — dominant retransition**.

This keeps the hook present in six of the eight bars either literally or through obvious fragments, so there is almost no opportunity for the listener to lose thematic identity.

## Looping, repetition fatigue, and browser implementation

A 19.2-second eight-bar loop at 100 BPM is short enough that its seam and internal repetition pattern will become perceptually important during prolonged reading. Research and practice in game audio consistently identify exact repetition as a potential source of fatigue or heightened awareness of the loop, which is why game-music systems often use variation, layering, or adaptive organization rather than unchanging repetition. citeturn446306search38turn446306search26 Kondo’s own test for game music has been repeated listening—he has described listening to candidate material over and over to determine whether it remains engaging without becoming tiring. citeturn254289search6

For this cue, however, the answer is not to make every cycle different. The cozy reading environment benefits from predictability. The better strategy is **low-information variation**: preserve melody and harmony while altering only one secondary parameter every few passes.

A practical four-cycle variation matrix is:

| Loop pass | Lead | Accompaniment | Ornament |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | normal | full light groove | none |
| B | normal | omit one inner layer in Bars 3–4 | tiny Bar-8 pickup |
| A | normal | full groove | none |
| C | normal | alternate bass octave in Bars 5–6 | one bell echo after D6 |

The listener therefore learns the 8-bar identity while the orchestration avoids becoming a mechanically identical 19.2-second object. This is consistent with game-audio research advocating variation as a means of disguising repetitive structure, and with Kondo’s emphasis on diversity among recurring motivic components. citeturn446306search38turn550548view0

Technically, a browser implementation should use the **Web Audio API’s audio clock**, not JavaScript timer callbacks as the ultimate timing authority. MDN explicitly recommends scheduling against `AudioContext.currentTime`, and its sequencing guidance uses look-ahead scheduling because ordinary execution timing can drift or be interrupted. citeturn446306search0turn446306search7

For a rendered 19.2-second loop, the most robust arrangement is an `AudioBufferSourceNode` with explicit `loopStart` and `loopEnd`. The Web Audio API defines those properties as exact offsets within the decoded audio buffer at which looping restarts. citeturn446306search1turn446306search3turn446306search4 Do not bake the 0.12-second release tail *after* the musical loop boundary if the tail changes the structural duration to 19.32 seconds; otherwise the pulse will slip perceptually on every repetition. Instead, render exactly 19.2 seconds of structural timeline and allow release/reverb to overlap the seam either through a continuously running effect bus or through separately scheduled overlapping voices.

In other words:

**Musical loop interval:** exactly 19.200 s.  
**Reverb/release:** may extend beyond 19.200 s.  
**Transport restart:** remains exactly on the next Bar 1.

That approach exploits the fact that the Web Audio graph can process sources and effects independently while scheduling playback at precise audio-context times. citeturn446306search5turn446306search6

For the actual seam, avoid three common errors. First, do not place a large crash, tonic bass hit, or long tonic chord on Bar 8 Beat 4; it announces the loop boundary. Second, do not allow an encoded silence pad before Bar 1. Third, do not fade the entire master bus in and out at the loop point. The musical arrangement should create the transition, while the engine merely repeats it accurately.

The ideal seam is therefore **harmonically asymmetrical but temporally exact**:

`Bar 8: … Em7 → A7(sus)`  
`loop boundary`  
`Bar 1: D(add9), melody F#–A–D`

The dominant belongs to the *end* of the stored loop; its tonic resolution belongs to the *beginning*. This ensures that every repetition contains a reason to cross the seam.

The broader compositional lesson from both phrase theory and game-music practice is that **a loop should not sound like a finished piece that happens to restart**. It should sound like a cyclic process whose strongest forward implication occurs precisely where the data wraps. Classical antecedent/consequent and sentential techniques provide the harmonic syntax for that implication; game scoring contributes timbral variation and accompaniment-led continuity; and browser audio scheduling preserves the temporal seam accurately. citeturn893769search0turn893769search9turn254289search5turn446306search0

For Lucky Ripple specifically, the highest-confidence design is therefore: retain the original two bars intact, animate the sustained D through bass and inner-voice motion, derive Bars 3–4 only from recognizable hook cells and end them on V, return the actual hook in Bars 5–6 under vi/IV-colored reharmonization, compress rather than invent material in Bar 7, and leave Bar 8 dominant-directed so that Bar 1—not Bar 8—provides the final tonic answer. This preserves the motif’s iconic octave-spanning contour while turning its problematic local closure into the stable anchor of a larger, continuously renewable eight-bar form. citeturn544936search3turn893769search2turn550548view0
