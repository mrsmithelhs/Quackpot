## What Suno Cover is actually doing—and what is not publicly known

Suno’s own product language is unusually explicit about the functional objective of Cover: it is intended to transform a source into a new style **“while keeping the melody.”** Suno also supports Cover directly on uploaded user audio, including simple recordings and instrument performances. By contrast, Suno’s Upload Audio documentation describes Extend as generating material from a chosen continuation point, which is a fundamentally different conditioning objective: Extend must establish continuity with an existing prefix, not restate the source melody. citeturn372252search1turn372252search6turn372252search3

What Suno does **not** disclose is the representation used internally. There is no Suno paper or technical documentation stating that Cover uses pitch contours, f0 tracking, MIDI-like transcription, chromagrams, semantic audio embeddings, codec tokens, diffusion latents, or a specific transformer architecture. The presence of an `audio_weight` control in third-party API wrappers shows that a scalar conditioning strength exists in the service’s generation metadata, but that does not reveal how the reference is encoded. citeturn560646search5

A useful comparison is MusicGen because its mechanism is public. MusicGen first represents audio with an EnCodec residual-vector-quantized tokenizer and generates streams of discrete acoustic tokens autoregressively. Text conditioning enters through learned conditioning tensors, while melody conditioning is based on a chromagram derived from reference audio. The researchers found that feeding the **raw chromagram** let the model reconstruct too much of the input, so they deliberately imposed an information bottleneck by retaining only the dominant pitch-class bin per time step. Their transformer then receives the melody representation as conditioning while still generating new acoustic tokens. citeturn701355view0turn946110view0

That result matters for interpreting Suno. It demonstrates why commercial systems need a tunable compromise between “copy this source” and “reinterpret this source”: a reference representation that retains too much information can collapse toward reconstruction, while one that retains too little permits melodic drift. Suno’s Audio Influence control is behaviorally consistent with changing the strength or effective weight of such a reference-conditioning path, although the exact implementation remains unknown. citeturn701355view0turn951881search2

The difference between **Cover** and **Extend** is therefore best understood as an objective difference, not merely a UI label. Cover asks the generator to create a new rendition *of* the conditioned source; Extend asks the generator to model *what follows* a source. Udio makes the same distinction explicitly: its documentation says Remix mirrors the underlying rhythmic and tonal structure, Style captures “vibe,” and Extend predicts what “should come next.” citeturn920129search0turn920129search1turn920129search2

For an exact hook that must survive orchestration, this strongly favors **Cover over Extend**. Extend may preserve key, tempo, harmonic character, instrumentation, or motifs statistically because those are useful for continuation, but it has no reason to replay a ten-note sequence exactly unless the model independently decides to do so. Cover is at least trained or product-tuned toward structural restatement. citeturn372252search0turn920129search1

## Audio Influence, Style Influence, and the practical fidelity frontier

Suno’s official slider documentation gives only qualitative semantics. **Weirdness** ranges from safe to chaotic, **Style Influence** controls adherence to text styling, and **Audio Influence** appears when audio has been uploaded. Suno does not publish a calibration curve mapping Audio Influence to pitch accuracy, note-edit distance, chroma similarity, or structural retention. citeturn372252search2turn951881search2

That means the useful numerical evidence comes from user testing and must be treated as anecdotal rather than controlled benchmarking. Several recent cover-focused users report that **85–95% Audio Influence**, very low Weirdness, and low-to-moderate Style Influence produce their closest results; one 2026 user reports that going beyond roughly 85% on v6 can “demolish” the source or create buggy output, while another v5.5-era user found the newer model tolerated 90% better than v5. Other users have observed that Cover can occasionally remain almost identical even at unexpectedly low Audio Influence, indicating that the slider is neither linear nor isolated from model state. citeturn560646reddit6turn560646reddit7turn560646reddit8turn560646reddit11

A defensible operational interpretation is therefore:

| Audio Influence | Expected behavior for your motif | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| ~50% | Reference is clearly present but text/style has enough freedom to rewrite intervals, phrase lengths, or rhythm | Moderate |
| ~70–80% | Strong melody anchor while retaining substantial arrangement freedom | Best initial operating region |
| ~80–90% | Maximum-fidelity search region; often strongest melodic retention but source-dependent | High practical value, less stable |
| ~95–100% | Can yield near-copy behavior, artifacts, or model instability; not reliably “better” than 85–90% | Experimental |

The table is a synthesis rather than an official Suno specification. Suno itself only states that turning Audio Influence up increases reference adherence, while creator evidence shows that the high end behaves nonlinearly and differently across model releases. citeturn951881search2turn560646reddit6turn560646reddit7turn560646search4

For **v6**, there is one additional control worth using: **Max Mode**. Suno specifically recommends Max Mode for covers that need to remain close to the source and for maintaining consistency through a song. Suno also states that the **Variety** control rewrites or adjusts style prompts; setting Variety to zero preserves direct control over the style description. For a pitch-critical experiment, v6 rather than v6-wild is the logical model because Suno describes standard v6 as the precision-oriented variant, whereas v6-wild is intentionally less predictable. citeturn951881search1turn951881search6turn951881search10

I would therefore begin your experiment at approximately **Audio 80%, Style 35–45%, Weirdness 0–10%, Variety 0, Max Mode on**. If the melody changes, move Audio upward in 5-point increments before changing anything else. If the result becomes sonically stiff, noisy, or too close to the raw reference, back Audio down slightly rather than adding more text. This is an experimental recommendation derived from Suno’s documented slider semantics plus current creator observations, not an official preset. citeturn951881search1turn951881search2turn560646reddit6turn560646reddit7

The existence of “artifacts” at high influence should also be understood carefully. There is no evidence that Suno literally copies waveform noise into the output in proportion to the slider. A more plausible interpretation is that high conditioning makes the generator reconcile incompatible constraints—reference timing/timbre versus new arrangement/style—and this conflict can manifest as strange timbres, doubled attacks, garbling, source-like residue, or unstable reconstruction. That interpretation is consistent with MusicGen’s finding that overly informative melody conditioning causes source reconstruction and with creator reports that very high Suno influence can produce awkward results. citeturn701355view0turn560646reddit7

## Reference-file design: timbre, density, and duration

For preserving a ten-note melodic contour, the most favorable source is likely a **dry monophonic pitched signal with clear attacks, no harmony, no drums, no stereo effects, and minimal reverb**. This is an engineering inference, but it follows directly from known melody-conditioning methods: when the target control signal is melodic, systems such as MusicGen intentionally discard timbral and polyphonic detail and retain pitch-class structure. Every additional event in a full arrangement—kick drums, chord tones, bass notes, cymbal energy, reverberation—adds potential information the encoder can treat as part of the reference. citeturn701355view0turn994307search2

Between your proposed timbres, I would rank them:

1. **Dry monophonic triangle or similarly simple synth with a strong fundamental and short, crisp envelope.**
2. **Dry piano or mallet/bell-like voice, provided sustain and overtones are controlled.**
3. **Full arrangement containing harmony, bass, percussion, and effects.**

This ranking is not because a triangle wave is known to be “Suno-optimal”—there is no such published evidence—but because it presents the intended event sequence with minimal ambiguity. A triangle wave still contains harmonic structure that helps pitch-related representations recognize pitch, yet it avoids the extreme complexity of a piano or ensemble signal. A very pure sine could theoretically be even simpler, but a slightly richer harmonic spectrum is generally a safer engineering reference for learned audio encoders than an unusually synthetic single-frequency tone. The latter conclusion is inferential rather than Suno-specific. citeturn701355view0

Piano is a reasonable second choice because its note attacks make rhythmic boundaries obvious, but each note contains a broad transient and many partials; a bell can be worse if its inharmonic partials or long decay cause overlapping pitch evidence. A full mix is the least desirable if the *only invariant you care about is the melody*, because it encourages the model to preserve or reinterpret several simultaneous musical dimensions at once. Suno explicitly demonstrates audio conditioning on drum patterns as well as pitched sources, which is evidence that Audio Influence is capable of following non-melodic temporal information too. citeturn951881search15turn372252search1

Your **9.72-second four-bar reference** is the duration I would use first. At 100 BPM in 4/4, each bar is 2.4 s; four bars are 9.6 s, and your 0.12 s tail gives 9.72 s. That comfortably clears Suno’s six-second minimum while providing the motif twice. citeturn372252search3turn372252search8

The repetition has two advantages. First, it provides two identical observations of the same pitch/rhythm pattern, making the hook statistically more salient. Second, it prevents the encoder from seeing a large amount of unrelated phrase development that could dilute the identity of the desired motif. This is an inference from conditioning principles; Suno has not published an ablation study on repeated versus unrepeated uploaded hooks. citeturn701355view0

I would **not begin with 19.32 seconds** unless the 9.72-second version proves unstable. Doubling the duration from four to eight bars does not add new information if all eight bars are identical repetitions; it merely increases the proportion of the reference context devoted to one repeated pattern. That may reinforce the motif, but it can also encourage the generator to infer a rigid four- or eight-bar source structure that it then tries to reproduce instead of freely arranging around the motif. No public Suno benchmark establishes a superiority point here, so 9.72 s is the cleaner first test. citeturn372252search4turn701355view0

An effective controlled test would compare **9.72 s × two motif statements** against **19.32 s × four motif statements** with everything else fixed and score each generated track by melodic accuracy rather than subjective quality. A simple metric would align the generated lead to the reference and calculate note-onset timing error, pitch-class accuracy, interval-sequence accuracy, and full 10-note exact-match rate. Because consumer music generators are stochastic, each condition should be run multiple times; otherwise a single lucky or unlucky seed can dominate the conclusion. This is experimental methodology rather than a platform claim.

## Prompt competition and how to minimize style-induced melodic drift

Modern conditional audio models often combine more than one conditioning signal. MusicGen provides a particularly clear published example: text is encoded into a conditioning tensor that the transformer can attend to, while melody information is supplied separately as a melodic conditioning representation. The generated audio tokens must satisfy both sources simultaneously. MusicGen’s authors explicitly evaluate the ability to remain aligned with the supplied melody while also obeying the text description. citeturn701355view0turn994307search1

This is why “text versus audio” should not be imagined as two literal vectors fighting in a single cross-attention matrix in every commercial system. That architecture is plausible, but Suno has not disclosed it. The broader phenomenon is nevertheless real: the sampler must generate one audio sequence that jointly satisfies potentially contradictory conditions. If the audio says “simple ten-note motif in D major” while the style text strongly implies “busy bebop piano, chromatic improvisation, frequent fills,” one of those constraints must give. citeturn701355view0

For your target—**cozy, playful game music**—the style prompt should therefore specify **arrangement and timbre without prescribing a new foreground melodic behavior**. The safest style prompt is short enough that it does not introduce competing musical structures:

> Cozy playful instrumental game soundtrack, D major, 100 BPM. Soft pizzicato strings, warm marimba, gentle acoustic bass, light brushed percussion, subtle woodwinds. Keep the supplied melody as the clear recurring lead motif. Simple supportive harmony, spacious arrangement, no melodic improvisation.

The core principle is to describe **instrumentation, production, role, density, and mood**, while avoiding terms such as *virtuosic, evolving melody, improvised lead, countermelody-heavy, intricate melodic development,* or any genre shorthand whose defining feature is melodic transformation. This recommendation follows from the multi-condition behavior demonstrated in research systems and Suno’s own description of Style Influence as adherence to the style input. citeturn701355view0turn951881search2

Suno also offers an official **Exclude** field. For this application, negative instructions should target the *failure modes* rather than restating the desired melody. Useful exclusions are:

> melodic improvisation, alternate melody, reharmonization, chromatic lead lines, dense countermelodies, rubato, tempo changes, key changes, vocalizations

Suno officially supports excluding instruments, styles, and other musical concepts through this field. citeturn951881search0

A particularly important distinction is between **harmonic freedom and melodic freedom**. You can let the model arrange richer accompaniment underneath the motif while still constraining the foreground line. If you instead write an extremely detailed structural prompt—intro, A section, B section, breakdown, modulation, bridge, development, final variation—you are telling the generator that musical evolution is important, which may increase the probability that the motif is altered as part of that evolution. This is an inference from conditional generation behavior rather than a disclosed Suno rule. citeturn701355view0

The most controlled process is therefore to raise **Audio Influence first**, keep **Weirdness near zero**, hold **Variety at zero**, and use only enough Style Influence to establish instrumentation. Suno’s current v6 documentation specifically notes that Variety alters style prompts, so leaving it active introduces another source of uncontrolled variation into an experiment whose dependent variable is melodic accuracy. citeturn951881search1

## Gemini/Lyria attachment routing and why your earlier result drifted

The current Gemini product has two separate capabilities that are easy to conflate. Gemini Apps can accept and **analyze audio files** in ordinary multimodal conversations; Google documents audio-upload limits of up to ten minutes for standard accounts and longer for some paid tiers. Separately, Gemini’s **Create music** feature invokes Lyria to synthesize music. citeturn692313search0turn692313search5

However, Google’s current Create Music instructions explicitly describe adding **images or videos** for musical context. They do not document WAV/audio-reference input to the music generator. Google’s Lyria 3.5 API model card is even more definitive: supported inputs are **Text and Image**, and output is Audio plus text/lyrics. Its music-generation API documentation likewise describes generation from text and images, not waveform-conditioned generation. citeturn692313search1turn692313search2turn692313search3

Lyria RealTime is also text-controlled rather than reference-audio-conditioned. Google exposes weighted text prompts plus controls such as guidance, BPM, density, brightness, scale, temperature, and top-k sampling, but no documented field for source waveform, chromagram, melody embedding, MIDI sequence, or audio-reference latent. citeturn694166search0turn694166search1

Therefore, the strongest conclusion supported by public documentation is: **there is no exposed Lyria audio-conditioning adapter in the Gemini consumer workflow or public Lyria 3.5 API as of October 31, 2026**. Google has not publicly described an internal path in which a WAV attached to Gemini is passed directly into Lyria’s generator as waveform conditioning. citeturn692313search1turn692313search3

What happens internally to an audio file attached in a normal Gemini conversation is not fully documented. Gemini can analyze the file, so the system may derive a textual or multimodal representation of its content; but saying that Gemini definitely transcribes the melody into text tokens and then feeds those exact tokens into Lyria would go beyond the available evidence. The important practical fact is that the public Lyria interface does **not** accept the waveform itself as a conditioning input. citeturn692313search0turn692313search3

That explains your empirical result without requiring any hidden architectural assumption. A multimodal model may correctly understand that the WAV is “a short bright melody in D major at roughly 100 BPM,” while still losing the exact interval and onset sequence when it communicates the musical intent to a text/image-conditioned generator. Semantic description is a far lower-bandwidth representation of a melody than explicit chroma, symbolic notes, or waveform conditioning. Systems such as MusicLM and MusicGen had to add dedicated melody-conditioning mechanisms precisely because text alone is not enough to encode an exact supplied melody. citeturn994307academia6turn701355view0

Google’s older MusicLM research is informative here. MusicLM explicitly demonstrated a dedicated melody-conditioning mode in which a hummed, whistled, sung, or instrumental melody could be transformed according to a text style prompt. That research proves Google has developed such technology, but the presence of a research capability does **not** mean the current Lyria 3.5 consumer endpoint exposes it. Current Lyria documentation lists text and image inputs only. citeturn994307search0turn692313search3

Use **Suno v6 Cover**, not v6-wild and not Extend, because Cover is explicitly melody-preserving and v6 is Suno’s precision-oriented model. Render the ten-note melody on a clean triangle-like or neutral synth voice with a short but non-clicking attack and enough sustain to make each pitch unambiguous. Make the four-bar file exactly **9.72 seconds**: two complete musical repetitions followed by the 120 ms tail.
